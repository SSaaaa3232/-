                - heading "1. Introduction" [level=2] [ref=e210]: 1. Introduction
                - paragraph [ref=e211]:
                  - text: (
                  - emphasis [ref=e212]: R
                  - text: )-citramalate is a dicarboxylic acid metabolite produced by microorganisms and plant cells. There is an industrial demand for (
                  - emphasis [ref=e213]: R
                  - text: )-citramalate as a precursor of methyl methacrylate (MMA). MMA is used as polymethyl methacrylate in various industrial products owing to its high clarity and impact resistance (
                  - link "Ali et al., 2015" [ref=e214] [cursor=pointer]:
                    - /url: "#bib1"
                  - text: ). The market economy of MMA grows every year and is expected to reach USD 8.16 billion by 2025 (
                  - link "Lebeau et al., 2020" [ref=e215] [cursor=pointer]:
                    - /url: "#bib24"
                  - text: ). The acetone cyanohydrin (ACH) route, which is the current method used for MMA synthesis, is an environmentally hazardous process that uses toxic reagents to produce many byproducts (
                  - link "Darabi Mahboub et al., 2018" [ref=e216] [cursor=pointer]:
                    - /url: "#bib14"
                  - text: ). However, the MMA synthesis method using (
                  - emphasis [ref=e217]: R
                  - text: )-citramalate as a precursor is attractive as an environmentally low-impact alternative to the ACH route because it uses a catalyst and water at high temperature and pressure, producing few byproducts and requiring no toxic reagents (
                  - link "Wu et al., 2021" [ref=e218] [cursor=pointer]:
                    - /url: "#bib46"
                  - text: ).
                - paragraph [ref=e219]:
                  - text: In a microbial metabolic reaction, 1 mol (
                  - emphasis [ref=e220]: R
                  - text: )-citramalate is synthesized from 1 mol pyruvate and 1 mol acetyl-CoA using citramalate synthase (EC 2.3.1.182). Several studies on the heterologous production of (
                  - emphasis [ref=e221]: R
                  - text: )-citramalate by microorganisms have been reported (
                  - link "Table 1" [ref=e222] [cursor=pointer]:
                    - /url: "#tbl1"
                  - text: ), mostly using
                  - emphasis [ref=e223]: Escherichia coli
                  - text: (
                  - link "Wu and Eiteman, 2016" [ref=e224] [cursor=pointer]:
                    - /url: "#bib44"
                  - text: ;
                  - link "Parimi et al., 2017" [ref=e225] [cursor=pointer]:
                    - /url: "#bib33"
                  - text: ;
                  - link "Webb et al., 2018" [ref=e226] [cursor=pointer]:
                    - /url: "#bib42"
                  - text: ;
                  - link "Wu et al., 2020" [ref=e227] [cursor=pointer]:
                    - /url: "#bib45"
                  - text: ","
                  - link "2023" [ref=e228] [cursor=pointer]:
                    - /url: "#bib47"
                  - text: ). However, the highly efficient production of (
                  - emphasis [ref=e229]: R
                  - text: )-citramalate by
                  - emphasis [ref=e230]: E. coli
                  - text: requires pH adjustment of the medium because of the low organic acid tolerance of
                  - emphasis [ref=e231]: E. coli
                  - text: . As the addition of neutralizers to the culture medium can increase the purification process after fermentation and product costs (
                  - link "Sauer et al., 2008" [ref=e232] [cursor=pointer]:
                    - /url: "#bib35"
                  - text: ;

                - paragraph [ref=e313]:
                  - text: Yeast is a promising microorganism for (
                  - emphasis [ref=e314]: R
                  - text: )-citramalate production because they are more acid- and alcohol-tolerant than
                  - emphasis [ref=e315]: E. coli
                  - text: is and are also sufficiently robust to withstand repeated fermentation and industrial use.
                  - link "Wu et al. (2023)" [ref=e316] [cursor=pointer]:
                    - /url: "#bib47"
                  - text: randomly integrated a citramalate synthase gene (
                  - emphasis [ref=e317]: cimA
                  - text: ) from
                  - emphasis [ref=e318]: Methanocaldococcus jannaschii
                  - text: into the chromosome of
                  - emphasis [ref=e319]: Issatchenkia orientalis
                  - text: ", a low-pH-tolerant non-model yeast, using a piggyBac transposon system and successfully produced 2.0 g/L ("
                  - emphasis [ref=e320]: R
                  - text: )-citramalate by batch fermentation.
                - paragraph [ref=e321]:
                  - text: Although the budding yeast,
                  - emphasis [ref=e322]: Saccharomyces cerevisiae
                  - text: ", has been well studied as a host for the heterologous production of useful compounds, to the best of our knowledge, there are no reports on its use in the production of ("
                  - emphasis [ref=e323]: R
                  - text: )-citramalate as a target compound, and there are only a few reports on alcohol production using (
                  - emphasis [ref=e324]: R
                  - text: )-citramalate as an intermediate (
                  - link "Shi et al., 2016" [ref=e325] [cursor=pointer]:
                    - /url: "#bib36"
                  - text: ;
                  - link "Nishimura et al., 2018" [ref=e326] [cursor=pointer]:
                    - /url: "#bib30"
                  - text: ).
                - paragraph [ref=e327]:
                  - text: As
                  - emphasis [ref=e328]: S. cerevisiae
                  - text: is not a natural producer of (
                  - emphasis [ref=e329]: R
                  - text: )-citramalate, it must heterologously express
                  - emphasis [ref=e330]: cimA
                  - text: . If
                  - emphasis [ref=e331]: cimA
                  - text: is expressed in the cytosol, the supply of both cytosolic pyruvate and cytosolic acetyl-CoA, which are substrates for (
                  - emphasis [ref=e332]: R
                  - text: )-citramalate synthesis, is important. However, the amount of acetyl-CoA in the yeast cytosol is thought to be less than that in
                  - emphasis [ref=e333]: E. coli
                  - text: because of the compartmentalization of intracellular acetyl-CoA by organelles. In previous research, the cytosolic acetyl CoA concentration in
                  - emphasis [ref=e334]: E. coli
                  - text: was estimated to range from 20 to 600 μM (
                  - link "Takamura and Nomura, 1988" [ref=e335] [cursor=pointer]:
                    - /url: "#bib40"
                  - text: ). In contrast, intracellular acetyl-CoA concentration in
                  - emphasis [ref=e336]: S. cerevisiae
                  - text: was estimated to be ∼30 μM, and one-third of the total acetyl-CoA pool was presumed to accumulate in the mitochondria. This suggests that the cytosolic acetyl-CoA concentration is lower than that in
                  - emphasis [ref=e337]: E. coli
                  - text: (
                  - link "Weinert et al., 2014" [ref=e338] [cursor=pointer]:
                    - /url: "#bib43"
                  - text: ). Organelles complicate metabolic engineering for high production from acetyl-CoA in the yeast cytosol, which is one of the fundamental challenges in the metabolic engineering of yeast (
                  - link "Krivoruchko et al., 2015" [ref=e339] [cursor=pointer]:
                    - /url: "#bib23"
                  - text: ). Cytosolic pyruvate, which is synthesized via glycolysis, can be converted by
                  - emphasis [ref=e340]: S. cerevisiae
                  - text: into acetyl-CoA using endogenous pyruvate decarboxylase, acetaldehyde dehydrogenase (ALD), and acetyl-CoA synthetase. To increase the amount of cytosolic acetyl-CoA in
                  - emphasis [ref=e341]: S. cerevisiae
                  - text: ", in addition to enhancing the endogenous cytosolic acetyl-CoA synthesis pathway, a common strategy is to construct heterologous pathways for acetyl-CoA supply ("
                  - link "van Rossum et al., 2016" [ref=e342] [cursor=pointer]:
                    - /url: "#bib41"
                  - text: ). For example, there are pathways that can convert acetaldehyde to acetyl-CoA via the pyruvate dehydrogenase (PDH) complex from
                  - emphasis [ref=e343]: E. coli
                  - text: or
                  - emphasis [ref=e344]: Enterococcus faecalis
                  - text: (
                  - link "van Rossum et al., 2016" [ref=e345] [cursor=pointer]:
                    - /url: "#bib41"
                  - text: ). As the endogenous and heterologous pathways listed above require NAD(P) or ATP, an appropriate pathway must be selected that does not compete with the cofactor demand associated with target compound synthesis.
                - paragraph [ref=e346]:
                  - text: In addition, inhibition of cytosolic acetyl-CoA and pyruvate transport to organelles may be effective in increasing the production of cytosolic acetyl-CoA.
                  - link "Cardenas and Da Silva (2016)" [ref=e347] [cursor=pointer]:

                  - text: One reason why reports on (
                  - emphasis [ref=e1221]: R

                  - emphasis [ref=e1222]: S. cerevisiae
                  - text: ", the presence of organelles makes it difficult to utilize cytosolic acetyl-CoA efficiently for the synthesis of target compounds. As mitochondria have higher concentrations of acetyl-CoA than the cytosol does ("
                  - link "Weinert et al., 2014" [ref=e1223] [cursor=pointer]:
                    - /url: "#bib43"
                  - text: ), they are often used as sites for the production of acetyl-CoA-derived compounds. However, the construction of pathways in mitochondria sometimes leads to the accumulation of metabolites, causing growth inhibition (
                  - link "Lv et al., 2016" [ref=e1224] [cursor=pointer]:
                    - /url: "#bib26"
                  - text: ;
                  - link "Yuan and Ching, 2016" [ref=e1225] [cursor=pointer]:
                    - /url: "#bib49"

                  - emphasis [ref=e1226]: S. cerevisiae

                - paragraph [ref=e1227]:
                  - text: We found that either Δ
                  - emphasis [ref=e1228]: POR1
                  - text: or Δ
                  - emphasis [ref=e1229]: YAT2
                  - text: slightly increased (
                  - emphasis [ref=e1230]: R
                  - text: )-citramalate production. Cytosolic acetyl-CoA is transported into the mitochondria in the form of acetyl-carnitine.                   - link "Franken et al., 2008" [ref=e1231] [cursor=pointer]:
                    - /url: "#bib18"

                  - emphasis [ref=e1232]: YAT2
                  - text: increased (
                  - emphasis [ref=e1233]: R

                - paragraph [ref=e1234]:

                  - link "Bricker et al., 2012" [ref=e1235] [cursor=pointer]:

                  - text: )-citramalate production. In fact, the final strain constructed, ICM-dYAT2-comp, produced 158 mM (7.3 g/L) ethanol and 16 mM (1.5 g/L) glycerol at 96 h of incubation in test tube culture (
                  - link "Supplementary Fig. 3" [ref=e1305] [cursor=pointer]:
                    - /url: "#appsec1"
                  - text: ). In
                  - emphasis [ref=e1306]: S. cerevisiae
                  - text: ", the synthesis of ethanol and glycerol plays a role in cytosolic NADH oxidation ("
                  - link "Bakker et al., 2001" [ref=e1307] [cursor=pointer]:
                    - /url: "#bib5"
                  - text: ). For example, the knockout of genes encoding acetaldehyde/alcohol dehydrogenase results in a significant reduction in growth due to an imbalance in redox cofactors and the accumulation of acetaldehyde, which subsequently leads to a decline in the productivity of target compounds (
                  - link "Song et al., 2016" [ref=e1308] [cursor=pointer]:
                    - /url: "#bib38"
                  - text: ). The synthesis of (
                  - emphasis [ref=e1309]: R
                  - text: )-citramalate does not oxidize excess NADH generated by deleting genes involved in ethanol or glycerol synthesis. Therefore, some manipulations are necessary to consume the toxic metabolic intermediate (
                  - link "Song et al., 2016" [ref=e1310] [cursor=pointer]:
                    - /url: "#bib38"
                  - text: ) and maintain intracellular redox balance (
                  - link "Yamada et al., 2017" [ref=e1311] [cursor=pointer]:
                    - /url: "#bib48"
                  - text: ) to achieve the high productivity of (
                  - emphasis [ref=e1312]: R
                  - text: )-citramalate with lower byproduct levels.
                - paragraph [ref=e1313]:
                  - text: (
                  - emphasis [ref=e1314]: R
                  - text: )-citramalate is an important compound not only as a precursor of MMA, but also as a metabolic intermediate in higher alcohol synthesis in microbial metabolic engineering (
                  - link "Shi et al., 2016" [ref=e1315] [cursor=pointer]:
                    - /url: "#bib36"
                  - text: ;
                  - link "Chen et al., 2017" [ref=e1316] [cursor=pointer]:
                    - /url: "#bib13"
                  - text: ). In other words, (
                  - emphasis [ref=e1317]: R
                  - text: )-citramalate has the potential to be an important intermediate for the synthesis of medium- and long-chain primary alcohols, which are industrially important compounds for biofuel production, in which the establishment of a high (
                  - emphasis [ref=e1318]: R
                  - text: )-citramalate production pathway in microorganisms is highly significant. Currently, 1-butanol, 1-pentanol, 1-hexanol, and 1-octanol production has been reported in engineered
                  - emphasis [ref=e1319]: E. coli
                  - text: strains (
                  - link "Zhang et al., 2008" [ref=e1320] [cursor=pointer]:
                    - /url: "#bib50"
                  - text: ;
                  - link "Dekishima et al., 2011" [ref=e1321] [cursor=pointer]:
                    - /url: "#bib16"
                  - text: ;
                  - link "Machado et al., 2012" [ref=e1322] [cursor=pointer]:
                    - /url: "#bib27"
                  - text: ;
                  - link "Chen et al., 2017" [ref=e1323] [cursor=pointer]:
                    - /url: "#bib13"
                  - text: ), whereas in
                  - emphasis [ref=e1324]: S. cerevisiae
                  - text: the synthesis of linear alcohols with more than five carbons has not been achieved (
                  - link "Shi et al., 2016" [ref=e1325] [cursor=pointer]:
                    - /url: "#bib36"
                  - text: ;
                  - link "Nishimura et al., 2018" [ref=e1326] [cursor=pointer]:
                    - /url: "#bib30"
                  - text: ). Higher alcohol synthesis in yeast remains a challenge because the carbon chain elongation reaction of the intermediate in this pathway requires acetyl-CoA.
                - paragraph [ref=e1327]:
                  - text: Although (
                  - emphasis [ref=e1328]: R
                  - text: )-citramalate production in yeast is much lower than that in
                  - emphasis [ref=e1329]: E. coli
                  - text: ", there is still potential for further enhancement of production levels. When the pH of culture decreases below the pKa of organic acid metabolites such as acetic acid and ("
                  - emphasis [ref=e1330]: R
                  - text: )-citramalate, undissociated organic acids that exist extracellularly readily cross the cell membrane and cause growth inhibition (Mira et al., 2010). The production of engineered
                  - emphasis [ref=e1331]: E. coli
                  - text: listed in
                  - link "Table 1" [ref=e1332] [cursor=pointer]:
                    - /url: "#tbl1"
                  - text: was obtained by adjusting the pH of the culture to 7.0 to avoid such acid stress. However, the recombinant yeasts constructed in this study did not require adjustment of culture pH. By closing the production gap with
                  - emphasis [ref=e1333]: E. coli
                  - text: through further metabolic pathway optimization and achieving low-pH organic acid production that has low economical and environmental impact (
                  - link "Bhagwat et al., 2021" [ref=e1334] [cursor=pointer]:
                    - /url: "#bib7"
                  - text: ), it would be advantageous to realize sustainable bioproduction. We hope that our findings on the metabolic engineering of a heterologous pathway in yeast to increase cytosolic acetyl-CoA levels will provide useful insights not only into (
                  - emphasis [ref=e1335]: R
                  - text: )-citramalate production, but also into achieving higher alcohol synthesis in yeast in the future.
              - generic [ref=e1336]:
                - heading "5. Conclusions" [level=2] [ref=e1337]: 5. Conclusions
                - paragraph [ref=e1338]:
                  - text: In this study, we reported, for the first time, the heterologous production of (
                  - emphasis [ref=e1339]: R
                  - text: )-citramalate by engineered
                  - emphasis [ref=e1340]: S. cerevisiae
                  - text: . To improve the supply of acetyl-CoA in the cytosol, which is required for (
                  - emphasis [ref=e1341]: R
                  - text: )-citramalate synthesis, we inhibited cytosolic pyruvate and acetyl-CoA transport to the mitochondria, established a PK/PTA pathway, and heterologously expressed relevant transporters. All our attempts had a positive effect on increasing (
                  - emphasis [ref=e1342]: R
                  - text: )-citramalate production, and combining them resulted in a large synergistic effect. The strain constructed in this study not only serves as a base strain for further optimization to construct (
                  - emphasis [ref=e1343]: R
                  - text: )-citramalate high-producing yeast, but also provides valuable insights into the highly efficient production of useful compounds from cytosolic acetyl-CoA, a challenge in the metabolic engineering of budding yeast.

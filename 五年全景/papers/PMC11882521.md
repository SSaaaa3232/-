                  - text: (L)-2,4-dihydroxybutyrate (DHB) is a versatile compound that can serve as a precursor for the synthesis of the methionine analog 2-hydroxy-4-(methylthio)butyrate and new advanced polymers. We previously implemented in

                  - text: an artificial biosynthetic pathway for the aerobic production of DHB from glucose, which relies on the deamination of (L)-homoserine followed by the reduction of 2-oxo-4-hydroxybutyrate (OHB) and yields DHB by an enzyme-bearing NADH-dependent OHB reductase activity. Under aerobic conditions, using NADPH as a cofactor is more favorable for reduction processes. We report the construction of an NADPH-dependent OHB reductase and increased intracellular NADPH supply by metabolic engineering to improve DHB production.                 - heading "1 Introduction" [level=2] [ref=e109]: 1 Introduction
                - paragraph [ref=e110]:
                  - text: (L)-2,4-dihydroxybutyrate (DHB) is a versatile compound of growing industrial relevance, as it can serve as a precursor for the chemical production of the methionine analog 2-hydroxy-4-(methylthio)butyrate (HMTB) used in animal nutrition (
                  - link "Walther et al., 2017a" [ref=e111] [cursor=pointer]:
                    - /url: "#B45"
                  - text: ) or as a building block for new advanced biopolymers (
                  - link "François, 2023" [ref=e112] [cursor=pointer]:
                    - /url: "#B19"
                  - text: ). Furthermore, DHB can serve as a precursor for the synthesis of 1,3-propanediol (
                  - link "Frazão et al., 2019" [ref=e113] [cursor=pointer]:
                    - /url: "#B22"
                  - text: ) or 1,2,4-butanetriol (
                  - link "Li et al., 2014" [ref=e114] [cursor=pointer]:
                    - /url: "#B31"
                  - text: ). Although the occurrence of DHB at trace levels in patients with succinic semialdehyde dehydrogenase deficiency has been described previously (
                  - link "Shinka et al., 2002" [ref=e115] [cursor=pointer]:
                    - /url: "#B43"
                  - text: ), there is no annotated natural metabolic pathway for its biosynthesis.
                - paragraph [ref=e116]:
                  - text: Aided by synthetic biology and enzyme engineering, we and others have previously reported three artificial biosynthetic pathways for the aerobic, microbial production of DHB starting from the widely abundant and inexpensive sugar glucose (
                  - link "Walther et al., 2017b" [ref=e117] [cursor=pointer]:
                    - /url: "#B47"
                  - text: ;
                  - link "Walther et al., 2015" [ref=e118] [cursor=pointer]:
                    - /url: "#B46"
                  - text: ;
                  - link "Walther et al., 2017a" [ref=e119] [cursor=pointer]:
                    - /url: "#B45"
                  - text: ). It is of note, however, that all the new routes are fully compatible with the use of other sugars (e.g., xylose, mannose, sucrose) or alcohols (e.g., methanol, ethylene glycol) as starting carbon sources, as all DHB pathways start from naturally occurring metabolites (homoserine, malate, or glyoxylate/acetyl-CoA). The different metabolic routes were tested in
                  - emphasis [ref=e120]: Escherichia coli.
                  - text: The highest reported titers (7.9 ± 0.01 mM) and yields (0.10 ± 0.01 mol
                  - subscript [ref=e121]: DHB
                  - text: mol
                  - subscript [ref=e122]: Glucose
                  - superscript [ref=e123]: −1
                  - text: ) in shake-flask cultivations with glucose as carbon source have been achieved with the DHB pathway proceeding through the characteristic natural intermediate (L)-homoserine (
                  - link "Frazão et al., 2018" [ref=e124] [cursor=pointer]:
                    - /url: "#B21"
                  - text: ). This route enables DHB synthesis via sequential deamination of (L)-homoserine by homoserine transaminase activity and reduction of 2-oxo-4-hydroxybutyrate (OHB) by an OHB reductase activity (
                  - link "Figure 1" [ref=e125] [cursor=pointer]:
                    - /url: "#F1"
                  - text: ). In a previous study, we reported the construction of the highly active NADH-dependent OHB reductase Ec.Mdh
                  - superscript [ref=e126]: 5Q
                  - text: (
                  - link "Frazão et al., 2018" [ref=e127] [cursor=pointer]:
                    - /url: "#B21"
                  - text: ). The mutant variant descends from the parent NAD
                  - superscript [ref=e128]: +
                  - text: "-dependent (L)-malate dehydrogenase from"
                  - emphasis [ref=e129]: E. coli
                  - text: (Ec.Mdh) and incorporates five mutations (I12V:R81A:M85Q:D86S:G179D) to yield the desired synthetic activity. However, the typical intracellular ratios of [NADH]/[NAD
                  - superscript [ref=e130]: +
                  - text: "] and [NADPH]/[NADP"
                  - superscript [ref=e131]: +
                  - text: "] in"
                  - emphasis [ref=e132]: E. coli
                  - text: cells cultivated under aerobic conditions are 0.03 and 60, respectively (
                  - link "Bennett et al., 2009" [ref=e133] [cursor=pointer]:
                    - /url: "#B2"
                  - text: ), which suggests that the utilization of NADPH as a cofactor in aerobic reduction processes may be more favorable. Therefore, employing an enzyme bearing NADPH-dependent OHB reductase activity could provide a strong advantage in terms of pathway performance.

                - paragraph [ref=e159]:
                  - text: In the absence of annotated naturally occurring enzymes with NADPH-dependent OHB reductase activity, we chose NADH-dependent Ec.Mdh
                  - superscript [ref=e160]: 5Q
                  - text: as the template enzyme to engineer the required activity. Previous studies have focused on engineering the nicotinamide cofactor specificity of oxidoreductases following rational approaches [as reviewed by
                  - link "Chánique and Parra (2018)" [ref=e161] [cursor=pointer]:
                    - /url: "#B8"
                  - text: "] and typically rely on introducing amino acid substitutions in the co-enzyme binding site. Following this logic, we used comparative sequence and structural analyses supported by the use of a structure-guided web tool ("
                  - link "Cahn et al., 2017" [ref=e162] [cursor=pointer]:
                    - /url: "#B7"
                  - text: ) to engineer NADPH-dependent OHB reductase activity. The best-performing mutant, assessed by
                  - emphasis [ref=e163]: in vitro
                  - text: analysis, was subsequently introduced into a strain that produced DHB via the synthetic homoserine pathway. To demonstrate the full potential of our engineered NADPH-dependent OHB reductase variant and to prevent NADPH becoming a limiting factor in product synthesis, we transferred the DHB pathway into NADPH-overproducing
                  - emphasis [ref=e164]: E. coli
                  - text: strains (
                  - link "Figure 1" [ref=e165] [cursor=pointer]:

                  - emphasis [ref=e166]: Ec.alaC
                  - subscript [ref=e167]:
                    - emphasis [ref=e168]: A142P:Y275D
                  - text: and showed a 50% increased DHB yield in shake-flask cultivations with glucose as the only carbon source for the most advanced strain.

                  - heading "3.5 Discussion" [level=3] [ref=e1136]
                  - paragraph [ref=e1137]:
                    - text: In previous work, we used a rational engineering approach to construct a highly active OHB reductase enzyme, catalyzing the last reaction step of the artificial homoserine-dependent DHB synthesis route (
                    - link "Frazão et al., 2018" [ref=e1138] [cursor=pointer]:
                      - /url: "#B21"
                    - text: ). Because we used the NAD
                    - superscript [ref=e1139]: +
                    - text: "-dependent"
                    - emphasis [ref=e1140]: E. coli
                    - text: malate dehydrogenase (Ec.Mdh; UniProtKB code
                    - link "P61889" [ref=e1141] [cursor=pointer]:
                      - /url: https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/protein/P61889
                    - text: ) as a template to construct Ec.Mdh
                    - superscript [ref=e1142]: 5Q
                    - text: (I12V:R81A:M85Q:D86S:G179D), the reduction of OHB in the synthetic pathway relied on NADH as a cofactor. However, typical intracellular ratios of [NAD(P)H]/[NAD(P)] in
                    - emphasis [ref=e1143]: E. coli
                    - text: (
                    - link "Bennett et al., 2009" [ref=e1144] [cursor=pointer]:
                      - /url: "#B2"
                    - text: ) indicate that the use of a NADPH-dependent OHB reductase provides a thermodynamic advantage. In this study, we aimed to replace the currently used NADH-dependent OHB reductase by an enzyme with NADPH-dependent activity.
                  - paragraph [ref=e1145]:
                    - text: Oxidoreductases with nicotinamide cofactor dependency exhibit a strong preference for either NAD(H) or NADP(H) (
                    - link "Chánique and Parra, 2018" [ref=e1146] [cursor=pointer]:
                      - /url: "#B8"
                    - text: ). In the large and complex (L)-malate/(L)-lactate dehydrogenase superfamily, most enzyme candidates of microbial origin rely on the NAD(H) cofactor system, typically showing only low residual activities with NADP(H) (
                    - link "Takahashi-Íñiguez et al., 2016" [ref=e1147] [cursor=pointer]:
                      - /url: "#B44"
                    - text: ;
                    - link "Brochier-Armanet and Madern, 2021" [ref=e1148] [cursor=pointer]:
                      - /url: "#B5"
                    - text: ). Whereas naturally existing NADP(H)-dependent Ldhs have not yet been identified (
                    - link "Richter et al., 2011" [ref=e1149] [cursor=pointer]:
                      - /url: "#B39"
                    - text: ), NADP(H)-preferring Mdhs exist in chloroplasts (
                    - link "Lemaire et al., 1996" [ref=e1150] [cursor=pointer]:
                      - /url: "#B29"
                    - text: ). Indeed, we initially envisaged the construction of a NADPH-dependent OHB reductase using the NADP
                    - superscript [ref=e1151]: +
                    - text: "-dependent chloroplast malate dehydrogenase originating from"
                    - emphasis [ref=e1152]: Sorghum bicolor
                    - text: (Sb.chMdhP) as a template enzyme (
                    - link "Crétin et al., 1990" [ref=e1153] [cursor=pointer]:
                      - /url: "#B14"
                    - text: ). Because we did not succeed in the production of a soluble, active enzyme variant in
                    - emphasis [ref=e1154]: E. coli
                    - text: ", we altered our strategy and instead focused on switching the cofactor specificity of previously reported NADH-dependent OHB reductase variant Ec.Mdh"
                    - superscript [ref=e1155]: 5Q
                    - text: toward NADPH.
                  - paragraph [ref=e1156]:
                    - text: Previous studies investigating the cofactor specificity of enzymes belonging to the (L)-malate/(L)-lactate dehydrogenase superfamily revealed that only a few amino acid residues are responsible for conferring the preference for either NAD(H) or NADP(H). In attempts to reverse the enzyme cofactor dependency, identifying crucial cofactor-discriminating key positions is usually followed by site-directed mutagenesis of the respective amino acid residues or a loop exchange approach (
                    - link "Chánique and Parra, 2018" [ref=e1157] [cursor=pointer]:
                      - /url: "#B8"
                    - text: ). Our present study confirms the crucial importance of the amino acid residues at positions 34 and 35 in Ec.Mdh in conferring cofactor preference. Upon replacing Asp34 and Ile35 with glycine and arginine, respectively, the cofactor specificity of the mutant Ec.Mdh
                    - superscript [ref=e1158]: 7Q
                    - text: was successfully altered from NADH to NADPH. Switching the cofactor specificity while maintaining comparable catalytic efficiency to that of the wild-type enzyme represents a significant challenge. In the majority of previous attempts of switching the cofactor specificity of oxidoreductases from NAD(H) to NADP(H), the catalytic efficiency of the mutant with NADP(H) was severely reduced, reaching less than 50% of the wild type’s catalytic efficiency with NAD(H) (
                    - link "Chánique and Parra, 2018" [ref=e1159] [cursor=pointer]:
                      - /url: "#B8"
                    - text: ). Although the catalytic efficiency of Ec.Mdh
                    - superscript [ref=e1160]: 7Q
                    - text: with NADPH was also reduced in the context of this study, it still reached more than 60% of that of the template enzymes with NADH. Further engineering efforts should be made to fully recover the activity of Ec.Mdh
                    - superscript [ref=e1161]: 7Q
                    - text: with NADPH compared to the template enzyme Ec.Mdh
                    - superscript [ref=e1162]: 5Q
                    - text: with NADH. More relevant for
                    - emphasis [ref=e1163]: in vivo
                    - text: DHB pathway operation using the NADPH-dependent variant, the overall catalytic efficiency for OHB reduction in the presence of the preferred cofactor defined as (k
                    - subscript [ref=e1164]: cat,app
                    - text: /K
                    - subscript [ref=e1165]: m,app
                    - text: )
                    - subscript [ref=e1166]: (OHB)
                    - text: × (k
                    - subscript [ref=e1167]: cat,app
                    - text: /K
                    - subscript [ref=e1168]: m,app
                    - text: )
                    - subscript [ref=e1169]: (NAD(P)H)
                    - text: of the mutant Ec.Mdh
                    - superscript [ref=e1170]: 7Q
                    - text: (on OHB with NADPH) was comparable to the template enzyme Ec.Mdh
                    - superscript [ref=e1171]: 5Q
                    - text: (on OHB with NADH). Thus, the engineered Ec.Mdh
                    - superscript [ref=e1172]: 7Q
                    - text: was a promising candidate for
                    - emphasis [ref=e1173]: in vivo
                    - text: application.

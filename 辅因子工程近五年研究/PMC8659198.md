                - paragraph [ref=e181]:
                  - text: Mitochondria are double-membrane organelles, and the majority of biological molecules including NAD cannot cross the inner membrane by diffusion. Therefore, mitochondria are thought to maintain a distinct NAD pool that is separated from the other parts of the cell. Although traditional enzymatic cycling assays and mass spectrometry analysis have been used to monitor whole-cell NAD pool and NAD
                  - superscript [ref=e182]: +
                  - text: /NADH ratios, they require homogenization of the cell or tissue (
                  - link "Zhao et al., 2011" [ref=e183] [cursor=pointer]:
                    - /url: "#bib44"
                  - text: ;
                  - link "Zou et al., 2018" [ref=e184] [cursor=pointer]:
                    - /url: "#bib48"
                  - text: ), cannot differentiate subcellular NAD pools, and are endpoint rather than real-time measurements. Optical imaging in live cells has been used to evaluate the autofluorescence of NADH, which is predominantly from the mitochondria but indistinguishable from the signal of reduced NAD phosphate (NADPH) (
                  - link "Huang et al., 2002" [ref=e185] [cursor=pointer]:
                    - /url: "#bib16"
                  - text: ). Autofluorescence imaging of NADH and flavin adenine dinucleotide has been used to determine the redox ratio in live cells, which correlated with the NAD
                  - superscript [ref=e186]: +
                  - text: /NADH ratio (
                  - link "Chance et al., 1979" [ref=e187] [cursor=pointer]:
                    - /url: "#bib5"
                  - text: ;
                  - link "Varone et al., 2014" [ref=e188] [cursor=pointer]:
                    - /url: "#bib33"
                  - text: ). The autofluorescence from cytosol and mitochondria can be separated by image segmentation (
                  - link "Bianchetti et al., 2021" [ref=e189] [cursor=pointer]:
                    - /url: "#bib2"
                  - text: ;
                  - link "Wallrabe et al., 2018" [ref=e190] [cursor=pointer]:
                    - /url: "#bib35"
                  - text: ). Nevertheless, autofluorescence measurement has critical limitations such as not being specific for mitochondria or NADH, not being a direct measurement of NAD
                  - superscript [ref=e191]: +
                  - text: /NADH ratio, low contrast and spatial resolution even under confocal or two-photon microscopy, and requiring complicated image processing and analysis. New approaches that can specifically monitor NAD dynamics and NAD
                  - superscript [ref=e192]: +
                  - text: /NADH redox status are needed to define the mechanistic roles of mitochondrial NAD in cell biology and pathology.

              - generic [ref=e518]:
                - heading "Discussion" [level=2] [ref=e519]: Discussion
                - paragraph [ref=e520]:
                  - text: In this study, we developed a mitochondrial-targeted genetic sensor (mt-SoNar) to assess the dynamic changes of NAD
                  - superscript [ref=e521]: +
                  - text: /NADH redox state in mitochondria under stress conditions. The subcellular compartment-targeted SoNar reveals previously unrecognized changes of NAD redox in mitochondria versus that in the cytosol, thus filling an important methodological gap for monitoring the compartmentalized NAD
                  - superscript [ref=e522]: +
                  - text: /NADH redox ratio in live cells and tissues, and
                  - emphasis [ref=e523]: in vivo
                  - text: . The study first tested mt-SoNar in cardiomyocytes because these cells are abundant in mitochondria, have a predominant mitochondrial NAD pool, and exhibit metabolic flexibility known to affect the mitochondrial redox state. We validated the results of mt-SoNar by using a well-accepted biochemical method for the mitochondrial NAD
                  - superscript [ref=e524]: +
                  - text: /NADH ratio based on the BDH reaction equilibrium (
                  - link "Figure S2" [ref=e525] [cursor=pointer]:
                    - /url: "#mmc1"
                  - text: ). The opposite responses of mt-SoNar to AcAc or β-OHB are also consistent with the BDH reaction. Moreover, mt-SoNar detected biphasic changes in mitochondrial NAD
                  - superscript [ref=e526]: +
                  - text: /NADH ratio during simulated ischemia reperfusion (
                  - link "Figure 1" [ref=e527] [cursor=pointer]:
                    - /url: "#fig1"
                  - text: H), which is consistent with the results of NAD(P)H autofluorescence (
                  - link "Zhang et al., 2018" [ref=e528] [cursor=pointer]:
                    - /url: "#bib42"
                  - text: ).
                  - emphasis [ref=e529]: In situ
                  - text: titration in permeabilized cells determined the direct response of mt-SoNar to a wide range of physiologically relevant NAD
                  - superscript [ref=e530]: +
                  - text: /NADH ratios (
                  - link "Chen et al., 2016" [ref=e531] [cursor=pointer]:
                    - /url: "#bib7"
                  - text: ). We further demonstrated that SoNar is capable of reporting NAD
                  - superscript [ref=e532]: +
                  - text: /NADH redox ratio in non-cardiomyocytes with a relatively small mitochondrial NAD pool, suggesting that compartment-targeted SoNar technology is broadly applicable to other cell types.
                - paragraph [ref=e533]:
                  - text: NAD
                  - superscript [ref=e534]: +
                  - text: and NADH are the most important redox pairs in the cell. Their ratio sets the intracellular redox environment, controls antioxidant capacity, and regulates cell signaling (
                  - link "Jones and Sies, 2015" [ref=e535] [cursor=pointer]:
                    - /url: "#bib17"
                  - text: ). The mitochondrial NAD
                  - superscript [ref=e536]: +
                  - text: /NADH ratio connects energy metabolism with crucial mechanisms for cell survival and function. For instance, NADH generated by oxidative metabolism provides reduction equivalent to NADPH for its antioxidant activity (
                  - link "Nickel et al., 2015" [ref=e537] [cursor=pointer]:
                    - /url: "#bib24"
                  - text: ). Compromised mitochondrial metabolism can decrease NADH generation, increase the NAD
                  - superscript [ref=e538]: +/
                  - text: NADH ratio, and impair antioxidant capacity, leading to oxidative stress, a mechanism underlying the contractile dysfunction of the failing heart (
                  - link "O'Rourke et al., 2021" [ref=e539] [cursor=pointer]:
                    - /url: "#bib25"
                  - text: ). On the other hand, a decrease in NAD
                  - superscript [ref=e540]: +
                  - text: /NADH ratio could reduce the availability of NAD
                  - superscript [ref=e541]: +
                  - text: for proteins requiring NAD
                  - superscript [ref=e542]: +
                  - text: as co-substrate, such as sirtuin deacetylase, and hence affect cell signaling (
                  - link "Karamanlidis et al., 2013" [ref=e543] [cursor=pointer]:
                    - /url: "#bib18"
                  - text: ;
                  - link "Lee et al., 2016" [ref=e544] [cursor=pointer]:
                    - /url: "#bib21"
                  - text: ). Recently it has been shown that serine can generate substantial NADH during mitochondrial respiration inhibition to further tilt the redox balance, leading to suppression of cell growth (
                  - link "Yang et al., 2020" [ref=e545] [cursor=pointer]:
                    - /url: "#bib40"
                  - text: ). It should be noted that in these studies, NAD
                  - superscript [ref=e546]: +
                  - text: and NADH are measured either indirectly via enzymatic assays or in whole-cell/tissue samples, and thus do not provide accurate information regarding the specific changes in subcellular compartments. The compartment-targeted SoNar technology, which possesses subcellular spatiotemporal resolution in live cells and without the liability of shifting redox state during destructive sample processing, is expected to provide a powerful tool for discovery.
                - paragraph [ref=e547]:
                  - text: The results of this study show that mitochondria of cardiomyocytes maintain a separate NAD pool with an NAD
                  - superscript [ref=e548]: +
                  - text: /NADH redox ratio distinct from that in the cytosol. The mitochondrial NAD
                  - superscript [ref=e549]: +
                  - text: /NADH ratio responded promptly to acute metabolic, physiological, or pathological perturbations as well as chronic adrenergic stress. However, normal mitochondria have the ability to sustain the NAD
                  - superscript [ref=e550]: +
                  - text: /NADH ratio under physiological stress conditions even during large increases of intracellular NAD
                  - superscript [ref=e551]: +
                  - text: level, due to a relatively larger NAD pool and a lower NAD
                  - superscript [ref=e552]: +
                  - text: /NADH ratio (
                  - link "Zhao et al., 2018" [ref=e553] [cursor=pointer]:
                    - /url: "#bib46"
                  - text: ). The mitochondrial NAD pool, although independently maintained, communicated with the cytosolic NAD pool via MAS. Specifically, in cells that rely primarily on mitochondrial metabolism, MAS may set the NAD
                  - superscript [ref=e554]: +
                  - text: /NADH homeostasis at resting conditions and shuttle NADH in or out of mitochondria to support elevated ETC activity or rebalance redox homeostasis. During acute and chronic pathological stress, the mitochondrial NAD
                  - superscript [ref=e555]: +
                  - text: /NADH ratio is affected more dramatically or much earlier in the disease progression than the cytosolic ratio, further supporting a predominant role of the mitochondrial NAD pool and redox status in defining the cellular responses to stress. Importantly, altered mitochondrial NAD
                  - superscript [ref=e556]: +
                  - text: /NADH ratio is not only a consequence of stress-induced metabolic responses but also a potential mechanism to mediate stress-induced cell damage and dysfunction. As such, the use of compartmentalized SoNar indicators, combined with manipulations of subcellular NAD
                  - superscript [ref=e557]: +
                  - text: /NADH ratios and functional assays, will be able to uncover novel targets and pathways by which the NAD
                  - superscript [ref=e558]: +
                  - text: /NADH redox ratio bridges a stress signal and a particular defect in cell function.
                - paragraph [ref=e559]:
                  - text: With the emerging interest in using NAD
                  - superscript [ref=e560]: +
                  - text: precursor supplementation as a therapy, the biological consequences of perturbing the intracellular NAD
                  - superscript [ref=e561]: +
                  - text: pool become a pressing question. We here reveal that the NAD
                  - superscript [ref=e562]: +
                  - text: /NADH redox ratio in mitochondria is affected only transiently in normal cardiomyocytes despite a large (>2-fold) increase in intracellular NAD
                  - superscript [ref=e563]: +
                  - text: by precursor supplementation. This finding is consistent with the notion that mitochondria redox is tightly controlled. While the ability of maintaining the mitochondrial NAD
                  - superscript [ref=e564]: +
                  - text: /NADH redox ratio is reassuring, it does not mean that increasing NAD
                  - superscript [ref=e565]: +
                  - text: would have no effect on mitochondria. Rather, it shows that mitochondria are able to respond and re-establish redox homeostasis, likely via increased conversion of NAD
                  - superscript [ref=e566]: +
                  - text: to NADH via oxidative metabolism. The cytosolic NAD
                  - superscript [ref=e567]: +
                  - text: /NADH redox ratio, however, remains elevated during NAD
                  - superscript [ref=e568]: +
                  - text: precursor supplementation even after the increase of cellular NAD
                  - superscript [ref=e569]: +
                  - text: plateaus. Thus, increased NAD
                  - superscript [ref=e570]: +
                  - text: level outside mitochondria could be an important mediator of the biological effects of NAD
                  - superscript [ref=e571]: +
                  - text: precursor supplementation (
                  - link "Canto et al., 2015" [ref=e572] [cursor=pointer]:
                    - /url: "#bib3"
                  - text: ;
                  - link "Yoshino et al., 2018" [ref=e573] [cursor=pointer]:
                    - /url: "#bib41"
                  - text: ). Further studies are needed to distinguish the mechanisms mediated by increased NAD
                  - superscript [ref=e574]: +
                  - text: level from that by altered NAD
                  - superscript [ref=e575]: +
                  - text: /NADH ratio.
                - paragraph [ref=e576]:
                  - text: In summary, we have developed a modified version of SoNar (mt-SoNar) that is genetically encoded and targeted into the mitochondrial matrix. Confocal imaging with mt-SoNar provides a specific signal of the mitochondrial NAD
                  - superscript [ref=e577]: +
                  - text: /NADH ratio and can be used in conjunction with non-targeted SoNar to distinguish NAD redox in mitochondrial versus cytosolic compartments. Our findings demonstrate the power of monitoring subcellular NAD redox in live cells. Deciphering the NAD redox regulation in mitochondria versus cytosol in cardiomyocytes provides novel insight into stress response, which whole-cell study fails to resolve.

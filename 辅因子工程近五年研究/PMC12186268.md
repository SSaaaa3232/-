                - heading "Abstract" [level=2] [ref=e103]: Abstract
                - paragraph [ref=e104]: Oxidoreductase enzymes are widely used biocatalysts due to their high enantioselectivity and broad substrate compatibility in useful transformations. Many oxidoreductases require nicotinamide cofactors (i.e., NAD(P)H). To replace this costly natural cofactor, synthetic nicotinamide cofactor biomimetics (NCBs) offer different shapes, binding affinities, and reducing potentials that exceed the capabilities of wild-type NAD(P)H. However, the ill-defined structure–activity relationships (SARs) of various NCBs slow rationally guided innovation, such as customized reducing potentials. Here, we dissect two essential elements of NCB design, holding the nicotinamide invariant. First, the linker length between the nicotinamide and an unconjugated aromatic ring uncovered unexpected benefits to redox activity for two or three carbon linkers. Second, substitution on this unconjugated aryl group (Ring 2) might not be expected to affect activity. However, SAR trends demonstrate substantial benefits to reductive potential conferred by electron-donating functionalities on Ring 2. Furthermore, catalysis by two enzymes demonstrates enzyme-dependent tolerance or sensitivity to the NCB structures. Density functional theory (DFT) and computational modeling provide a theoretical framework to understand and build upon these observations. Ring 2 reaches up to the nicotinamide to stabilize its positive charge after oxidation through π–π stacking and charge transfer. Thus, the systematic examination of NCB’s stability, electrochemical redox potentials, and kinetics uncovers trends for the improved design of NCBs.

              - paragraph [ref=e128]:
                - text: To address the gap between capabilities and applications, researchers have developed synthetic analogs to NAD(P)/NAD(P)H, termed nicotinamide cofactor biomimetics (NCBs). These analogs can decrease cofactor cost and have also been shown to enhance biocatalytic activity.
                - emphasis [ref=e129]: ","
                - text: To date, NCBs have been used as cofactors in catalysis by diaphorases, ene-reductases, cytochrome P450s, dehydrogenases, oxidases, monoxygenases, azoreductases, and hydroxylases.
              - paragraph [ref=e130]:
                - text: The structure of NAD
                - superscript [ref=e131]: +
                - text: /NAD(P)H offers several sites for modification, while maintaining an intact hydride donating and accepting moiety (
                - link "Scheme" [ref=e132] [cursor=pointer]:
                  - /url: "#sch1"
                - text: ). Among the plethora of possible nicotinamide modifications, the carboxamide of the nicotinamide offers a simple strategy for modulating the cofactor’s reductive potential. The nicotinamide amide can be readily hydrolyzed to a carboxylic acid; the resultant NCB has a greater reducing potential. Alternatively, replacing the amide with an acetyl group decreases the cofactor’s reducing potential. Modification of the nicotinamide maintains a large part of the cofactor’s structure, and thus can preserve conventional active site recognition. Such recognition can allow native enzymes to accept these cofactors with little protein engineering. However, this modification does not decrease cofactor cost, does not address atom economy concerns, and can require extensive synthetic routes to access (e.g., to exchange the carboxamide for an acetyl group).
              - paragraph [ref=e133]:
                - text: Conversely, modification of the nicotinamide cofactor tail can address the above concerns and tailor its activity. Additionally, the approach can provide synthetic access to a large library of NCB derivatives (
                - link "Figure" [ref=e134] [cursor=pointer]:
                  - /url: "#fig1"
                - text: ). However, this dramatic simplification of cofactor structure can cause loss of molecular recognition by the cofactor’s native, wild-type enzymes. Thus, such NCBs can require extensive enzyme engineering to accommodate the new NCB structure. The exceptions are flavin-mediated enzymes, which can often tolerate NCBs.

                - heading "Conclusions" [level=2] [ref=e593]: Conclusions
                - paragraph [ref=e594]: NCBs offer simpler, less costly alternatives to replace NAD(P)H. As illustrated here, the synthetic accessibility and overall good synthetic yields of NCBs allow for robust diversification of cofactor variants. In this report, we demonstrate that the new NCBs can perform better than the wild-type NAD(P)H; performance metrics for reductive potential, for example, exceeded the native cofactor in three environmentsat an electrode, in solution, and in an enzyme active site. Furthermore, the electrochemical potentials, hydride transferability, and enzyme-catalyzed electron transfer reaction reported here demonstrate clear trends in NCB SARs.
                - paragraph [ref=e595]: This report investigated substitutions on NCB Ring 2. NCBs furnished with an electron-donating group exhibit enhanced reduction capability. Conversely, the addition of an electron-withdrawing group tends to diminish its reductive activity, as measured here by five assays. As shown by DFT calculations, the trends in activity can be explained by stabilizing interactions of the electron rich Ring 2 with the nicotinamide ring upon oxidation. This interaction favors hydride donation to drive dihydro-nicotinamide oxidation. The electron-poor Ring 2 structure, however, destabilizes the oxidized nicotinamide ring to disfavor hydride donation during oxidation. The countertrend in NCB stability described here could cap reductive potential of NCBs for aqueous solutions.
                - paragraph [ref=e596]:
                  - text: Additionally, extension of the carbon chain linker between the nicotinamide and Ring 2 results in unexpected gains in reduction capabilities. Extending this linker enhanced the NCB’s activity significantly in electrode-, solution-, and enzyme-based assays. The best and worst performing NCBs retained their rankings in the
                  - emphasis [ref=e597]: Gs
                  - text: DI activity assay. Thus, we conclude that the
                  - emphasis [ref=e598]: Gs
                  - text: DI can accommodate the NCB structures described in
                  - link "Table" [ref=e599] [cursor=pointer]:
                    - /url: "#tbl3"
                  - text: and
                  - link "Figure" [ref=e600] [cursor=pointer]:
                    - /url: "#fig7"
                  - text: . Furthermore, the toolkit of assays described here can report useful information about performance of NCBs in
                  - emphasis [ref=e601]: Gs
                  - text: DI. Future experiments could explore the generality of this conclusion. Testing with the second enzyme, NTR, revealed that the SAR of the NCBs is dependent on fit in the active site. Thus, maintaining enhanced activity of the more reducing NCBs could require enzyme engineering for enzymes with more specific active site requirements.
                - paragraph [ref=e602]: The NCB SAR trends in reductive activities could guide the development of NCBs with even more hyper-functional capabilities. For example, a collection of NCBs could be used in conjunction with a collection of enzymes to reduce higher barrier substrates. Such reductions typically rely heavily on toxic and dangerous to handle reducing agents. The aforementioned capabilities could therefore deliver on the promise of NCBs for greener, safer chemical processes.

                  - paragraph [ref=e676]: Density functional theory (DFT) was used for all electronic structure calculations.

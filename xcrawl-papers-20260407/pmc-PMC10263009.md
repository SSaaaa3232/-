                - heading "Abstract" [level=2] [ref=e111]: Abstract
                - paragraph [ref=e112]:
                  - text: Xylose is the second most abundant monomeric sugar in plant biomass. Consequently, xylose catabolism is an ecologically important trait for saprotrophic organisms, as well as a fundamentally important trait for industries that hope to convert plant mass to renewable fuels and other bioproducts using microbial metabolism. Although common across fungi, xylose catabolism is rare within Saccharomycotina, the subphylum that contains most industrially relevant fermentative yeast species. The genomes of several yeasts unable to consume xylose have been previously reported to contain the full set of genes in the
                  - emphasis [ref=e113]: XYL
                  - text: pathway, suggesting the absence of a gene–trait correlation for xylose metabolism. Here, we measured growth on xylose and systematically identified
                  - emphasis [ref=e114]: XYL
                  - text: pathway orthologs across the genomes of 332 budding yeast species. Although the
                  - emphasis [ref=e115]: XYL
                  - text: pathway coevolved with xylose metabolism, we found that pathway presence only predicted xylose catabolism about half of the time, demonstrating that a complete
                  - emphasis [ref=e116]: XYL
                  - text: pathway is necessary, but not sufficient, for xylose catabolism. We also found that
                  - emphasis [ref=e117]: XYL1
                  - text: copy number was positively correlated, after phylogenetic correction, with xylose utilization. We then quantified codon usage bias of
                  - emphasis [ref=e118]: XYL
                  - text: genes and found that
                  - emphasis [ref=e119]: XYL3
                  - text: codon optimization was significantly higher, after phylogenetic correction, in species able to consume xylose. Finally, we showed that codon optimization of
                  - emphasis [ref=e120]: XYL2
                  - text: was positively correlated, after phylogenetic correction, with growth rates in xylose medium. We conclude that gene content alone is a weak predictor of xylose metabolism and that using codon optimization enhances the prediction of xylose metabolism from yeast genome sequence data.

              - generic [ref=e124]:
                - heading "Introduction" [level=2] [ref=e125]: Introduction
                - paragraph [ref=e126]:
                  - text: Xylose is the most abundant pentose sugar and the second most abundant monomeric sugar in plant biomass, second only to glucose. Xylose occurs in xylan polymers in hemicellulose; therefore, the ability to hydrolyze xylan and oxidize xylose for energy is a common trait in saprophytic fungi (
                  - link "Polizeli et al. 2005" [ref=e127] [cursor=pointer]:
                    - /url: "#msad111-B45"
                  - text: ). Metabolic conversion of xylose is also a key process in the efficient conversion of lignocellulosic biomass into biofuels and other bioproducts via fermentation by industrially leveraged yeast species. Unlike filamentous fungi, native xylose assimilation appears to be a somewhat rare trait within budding yeasts.
                  - emphasis [ref=e128]: Saccharomyces cerevisiae
                  - text: is the choice microbe for the industrial production of the vast majority of biofuels due to its high ethanol tolerance, high glycolytic and fermentative capacity, and amenability to genetic engineering (
                  - link "Hong and Nielsen 2012" [ref=e129] [cursor=pointer]:
                    - /url: "#msad111-B19"
                  - text: ). However,
                  - emphasis [ref=e130]: S. cerevisiae
                  - text: requires genetic engineering to metabolize xylose, and even engineered strains are often inefficient in the fermentation of lignocellulosic xylose (
                  - link "Osiro et al. 2019" [ref=e131] [cursor=pointer]:
                    - /url: "#msad111-B42"
                  - text: ;
                  - link "Lee, Tremaine et al. 2021" [ref=e132] [cursor=pointer]:
                    - /url: "#msad111-B32"
                  - text: ;
                  - link "Lee, Yook et al. 2021" [ref=e133] [cursor=pointer]:
                    - /url: "#msad111-B33"
                  - text: ;
                  - link "Sun and Jin 2021" [ref=e134] [cursor=pointer]:
                    - /url: "#msad111-B59"
                  - text: ). This has led to the suggestion that cost-effective industrial conversion of xylose would be better achieved using native pentose-fermenting yeast species. One successful approach to identifying xylolytic species is the isolation of yeasts from xylose-rich environments, such as rotting logs and the guts of wood-boring beetles (
                  - link "Nguyen et al. 2006" [ref=e135] [cursor=pointer]:
                    - /url: "#msad111-B40"
                  - text: ;
                  - link "Cadete et al. 2012" [ref=e136] [cursor=pointer]:
                    - /url: "#msad111-B9"
                  - text: ;
                  - link "Urbina et al. 2013" [ref=e137] [cursor=pointer]:
                    - /url: "#msad111-B63"
                  - text: ). Given that budding yeast genomes are increasingly available (
                  - link "Riley et al. 2016" [ref=e138] [cursor=pointer]:
                    - /url: "#msad111-B49"
                  - text: ;
                  - link "Shen et al. 2018" [ref=e139] [cursor=pointer]:
                    - /url: "#msad111-B55"
                  - text: ), a simpler means of identifying xylolytic yeasts through genome sequence data would facilitate the discovery of additional xylose-metabolizing yeasts.
                - paragraph [ref=e140]:
                  - text: The budding yeast xylose catabolism pathway was first described in
                  - emphasis [ref=e141]: Cyberlindnera jadinii
                  - text: and
                  - emphasis [ref=e142]: Candida albicans
                  - text: (
                  - link "Chiang and Knight 1960" [ref=e143] [cursor=pointer]:
                    - /url: "#msad111-B11"
                  - text: ;
                  - link "Veiga et al. 1960" [ref=e144] [cursor=pointer]:
                    - /url: "#msad111-B64"
                  - text: ;
                  - link "Chakravorty et al. 1962" [ref=e145] [cursor=pointer]:
                    - /url: "#msad111-B10"
                  - text: ), but most subsequent characterization has focused on xylose-fermenting genera, including
                  - emphasis [ref=e146]: Scheffersomyces
                  - text: and, more recently,
                  - emphasis [ref=e147]: Spathaspora
                  - text: (
                  - link "Verduyn et al. 1985" [ref=e148] [cursor=pointer]:
                    - /url: "#msad111-B65"
                  - text: ;
                  - link "Kötter et al. 1990" [ref=e149] [cursor=pointer]:
                    - /url: "#msad111-B28"
                  - text: ;
                  - link "Cadete et al. 2016" [ref=e150] [cursor=pointer]:
                    - /url: "#msad111-B8"
                  - text: "). The native enzymatic pathway consists of three genes:"
                  - emphasis [ref=e151]: XYL1
                  - text: ","
                  - emphasis [ref=e152]: XYL2
                  - text: ", and"
                  - emphasis [ref=e153]: XYL3
                  - text: .
                  - emphasis [ref=e154]: XYL1
                  - text: and
                  - emphasis [ref=e155]: XYL2
                  - text: encode a xylose reductase (XR) and xylitol dehydrogenase (XDH), respectively, which function in the oxidoreductive conversion of xylose to xylulose with xylitol as an intermediate.
                  - emphasis [ref=e156]: XYL3
                  - text: encodes a xylulokinase, which phosphorylates xylulose to xylulose-5-phosphate to be fed into the nonoxidative branch of the pentose phosphate pathway (PPP). The identification of yeasts with complete pathways that were nonetheless unable to grow on xylose in previous surveys suggests a weak or absent gene–trait association between complete
                  - emphasis [ref=e157]: XYL
                  - text: pathways and xylose assimilation traits (
                  - link "Wohlbach et al. 2011" [ref=e158] [cursor=pointer]:
                    - /url: "#msad111-B68"
                  - text: ;
                  - link "Riley et al. 2016" [ref=e159] [cursor=pointer]:
                    - /url: "#msad111-B49"
                  - text: ).
                - paragraph [ref=e160]:
                  - text: In addition to a complete
                  - emphasis [ref=e161]: XYL
                  - text: pathway, other genetic and regulatory features may be important in determining xylose metabolic traits. Most studies have focused on the role of redox imbalance, which is thought to be produced by the different cofactor preferences of XR and XDH due to their preferences for NADPH and NAD
                  - superscript [ref=e162]: +
                  - text: ", respectively ("
                  - link "Bruinenberg et al. 1983" [ref=e163] [cursor=pointer]:
                    - /url: "#msad111-B6"
                  - text: ). This hypothesis is supported by the observation that some well-studied yeasts that efficiently metabolize xylose have evolved XR enzymes able to use NADH in addition to or in lieu of NADPH (
                  - link "Bruinenberg et al. 1984" [ref=e164] [cursor=pointer]:
                    - /url: "#msad111-B7"
                  - text: ;
                  - link "Schneider et al. 1989" [ref=e165] [cursor=pointer]:
                    - /url: "#msad111-B53"
                  - text: ;
                  - link "Cadete et al. 2016" [ref=e166] [cursor=pointer]:
                    - /url: "#msad111-B8"
                  - text: ). Recently, it has been suggested that changes to cofactor preference in methylglyoxal reductase (encoded by
                  - emphasis [ref=e167]: GRE2
                  - text: ) may also alleviate redox imbalance in xylofermentative yeasts (
                  - link "Borelli et al. 2019" [ref=e168] [cursor=pointer]:
                    - /url: "#msad111-B5"
                  - text: ). Additional properties, such as transporter presence or copy number and the expression of other metabolic genes, have also been implicated in xylose utilization (
                  - link "Wohlbach et al. 2011" [ref=e169] [cursor=pointer]:
                    - /url: "#msad111-B68"
                  - text: ). It is difficult to say how broadly applicable any of these explanations may be because the presence of
                  - emphasis [ref=e170]: XYL
                  - text: genes in the absence of xylose catabolism has only been studied in a handful of related yeast species. Thus, we do not know the extent of this lack of association across budding yeasts and whether other genome characteristics would enhance predictions concerning xylose metabolism.
                - paragraph [ref=e171]:
                  - text: The identification of some yeasts with complete
                  - emphasis [ref=e172]: XYL
                  - text: pathways that lack xylose assimilation suggests that xylose utilization may be much more difficult to predict based on gene content than many other metabolic traits, such as galactose utilization (
                  - link "Riley et al. 2016" [ref=e173] [cursor=pointer]:
                    - /url: "#msad111-B49"
                  - text: ;
                  - link "Shen et al. 2018" [ref=e174] [cursor=pointer]:
                    - /url: "#msad111-B55"
                  - text: ). An alternative strategy to predicting metabolic traits from gene content is evaluating specific metabolic genes for evidence of selection. Measuring selection on codon usage is one such approach. Among metrics developed to measure codon usage bias (
                  - link "Bennetzen and Hall 1982" [ref=e175] [cursor=pointer]:
                    - /url: "#msad111-B2"
                  - text: ;
                  - link "Sharp and Li 1987" [ref=e176] [cursor=pointer]:
                    - /url: "#msad111-B54"
                  - text: ;
                  - link "Wright 1990" [ref=e177] [cursor=pointer]:
                    - /url: "#msad111-B70"
                  - text: ), codon optimization captures how well matched individual codons are to their respective tRNA copy numbers in a given genome (
                  - link "dos Reis et al. 2004" [ref=e178] [cursor=pointer]:
                    - /url: "#msad111-B12"
                  - text: ). Accordingly, a codon with a low-copy corresponding tRNA is less optimized than a codon with a high-copy corresponding tRNA. The codon optimization index of a gene therefore measures the concordance between its transcript and the cellular tRNA pool and has repeatedly been shown to correlate with gene expression levels (
                  - link "Gouy and Gautier 1982" [ref=e179] [cursor=pointer]:
                    - /url: "#msad111-B16"
                  - text: ;
                  - link "Duret and Mouchiroud 1999" [ref=e180] [cursor=pointer]:
                    - /url: "#msad111-B13"
                  - text: ;
                  - link "Zhou et al. 2016" [ref=e181] [cursor=pointer]:
                    - /url: "#msad111-B72"
                  - text: ). Recent work has shown that codon usage is under translational selection in most fungal species (
                  - link "Wint et al. 2022" [ref=e182] [cursor=pointer]:
                    - /url: "#msad111-B67"
                  - text: ), including within budding yeasts (
                  - link "Labella et al. 2019" [ref=e183] [cursor=pointer]:
                    - /url: "#msad111-B30"
                  - text: ). Studies examining the relationship between codon usage and metabolism in fungi have found that codon bias is elevated in genes encoding important metabolic pathways (
                  - link "Gonzalez et al. 2020" [ref=e184] [cursor=pointer]:
                    - /url: "#msad111-B15"
                  - text: ), and, further, that codon optimization of metabolic genes is predictive of growth in corresponding conditions (
                  - link "LaBella et al. 2021" [ref=e185] [cursor=pointer]:
                    - /url: "#msad111-B31"
                  - text: ). Codon optimization of xylolytic genes has not been studied, but we hypothesize that it may be more useful than gene content in predicting which budding yeast species are well adapted to xylose metabolism.
                - paragraph [ref=e186]:
                  - text: Here, we measure growth on xylose and systematically identify
                  - emphasis [ref=e187]: XYL
                  - text: pathway orthologs across 332 publicly available budding yeast genomes (
                  - link "Shen et al. 2018" [ref=e188] [cursor=pointer]:
                    - /url: "#msad111-B55"
                  - text: ). In agreement with previous work, we find that an intact
                  - emphasis [ref=e189]: XYL
                  - text: pathway often does not confer xylose assimilation. We find multicopy
                  - emphasis [ref=e190]: XYL1
                  - text: and
                  - emphasis [ref=e191]: XYL2
                  - text: lineages to be common, and we find support for the hypothesis that
                  - emphasis [ref=e192]: XYL
                  - text: gene copy number is important by showing that
                  - emphasis [ref=e193]: XYL1
                  - text: copy number coevolves with the ability to consume xylose. We then generate codon optimization indices for all
                  - emphasis [ref=e194]: XYL
                  - text: homologs and show that
                  - emphasis [ref=e195]: XYL3
                  - text: codon optimization is significantly correlated with the ability to consume xylose, whereas codon optimization of
                  - emphasis [ref=e196]: XYL2
                  - text: is significantly positively correlated with kinetic growth rates on xylose. Collectively, our analyses reveal two genomic properties, copy number of
                  - emphasis [ref=e197]: XYL1
                  - text: and codon optimization of
                  - emphasis [ref=e198]: XYL2
                  - text: and
                  - emphasis [ref=e199]: XYL3
                  - text: ", that correlate with xylose metabolism and can be used as novel means of predicting xylolytic traits from genome sequence alone."

                - heading "Discussion" [level=2] [ref=e451]: Discussion
                - paragraph [ref=e452]:
                  - text: Xylose fermentation is an ecologically important trait of immense biotechnological value for the conversion of sustainable plant feedstocks into biofuels. This study identifies systematically
                  - emphasis [ref=e453]: XYL
                  - text: pathway homologs across a wide breadth of Saccharomycotina that includes representative species from all 12 major clades. Whereas most genomes examined contain complete pathways, less than half of those species were able to assimilate xylose under laboratory conditions. This stands in contrast to other metabolic traits that have been investigated in yeasts that exhibit strong gene–trait associations (
                  - link "Riley et al. 2016" [ref=e454] [cursor=pointer]:
                    - /url: "#msad111-B49"
                  - text: ;
                  - link "Shen et al. 2018" [ref=e455] [cursor=pointer]:
                    - /url: "#msad111-B55"
                  - text: ). For example, a survey of galactose metabolism across the same extensive collection of budding yeast species found that 89% of species with complete
                  - emphasis [ref=e456]: GAL
                  - text: pathways were able to use galactose as a carbon source in the laboratory (
                  - link "LaBella et al. 2021" [ref=e457] [cursor=pointer]:
                    - /url: "#msad111-B31"
                  - text: ). The poor ability of gene content to predict xylose–metabolism traits has been noted before in surveys of a small number of biotechnologically important yeasts (
                  - link "Wohlbach et al. 2011" [ref=e458] [cursor=pointer]:
                    - /url: "#msad111-B68"
                  - text: ;
                  - link "Riley et al. 2016" [ref=e459] [cursor=pointer]:
                    - /url: "#msad111-B49"
                  - text: ), but it was unclear whether this limited gene–trait association would apply broadly across budding yeasts. Whereas complete pathways are found in all major yeast clades, xylose metabolism is variable; most CUG-Ser1 species are able to utilize xylose, assimilation shows up sporadically in most other clades, and it is completely absent in the Saccharomycetaceae. These patterns are consistent with previous observations (reviewed in
                  - link "Ruchala and Sibirny (2021)" [ref=e460] [cursor=pointer]:
                    - /url: "#msad111-B51"
                  - text: ).
                - paragraph [ref=e461]:
                  - text: One limitation of this study and a possible explanation for the poor correlation between genotype and phenotype is that xylose catabolism requires specific conditions. We analyzed only growth data generated in our assay under a single laboratory condition. For some species, our data conflict with data aggregated from species descriptions (
                  - link "Opulente et al. 2018" [ref=e462] [cursor=pointer]:
                    - /url: "#msad111-B41"
                  - text: ). For other species, conflicting data also exist elsewhere in the literature. For example,
                  - emphasis [ref=e463]: Kluyveromyces marxianus
                  - text: did not grow in our 96-well plate assay but has been found to consume xylose in shake flasks (
                  - link "Margaritis and Bajpai 1982" [ref=e464] [cursor=pointer]:
                    - /url: "#msad111-B37"
                  - text: ). Oxygenation, base media, and temperature have all been documented as affecting xylose metabolism in different yeast species (
                  - link "Signori et al. 2014" [ref=e465] [cursor=pointer]:
                    - /url: "#msad111-B56"
                  - text: ;
                  - link "Osiro et al. 2019" [ref=e466] [cursor=pointer]:
                    - /url: "#msad111-B42"
                  - text: ). Beyond condition dependence, intraspecific metabolic heterogeneity, such as is known to occur in
                  - emphasis [ref=e467]: Kluyveromyces lactis
                  - text: and
                  - emphasis [ref=e468]: Torulaspora delbrueckii
                  - text: ", could also produce inconsistencies ("
                  - link "Lyutova et al. 2021" [ref=e469] [cursor=pointer]:
                    - /url: "#msad111-B36"
                  - text: ;
                  - link "Silva et al. 2023" [ref=e470] [cursor=pointer]:
                    - /url: "#msad111-B57"
                  - text: ). A final reason why our data may conflict with preexisting descriptions is historical human errors in species typing and identification (
                  - link "Haase et al. 2017" [ref=e471] [cursor=pointer]:
                    - /url: "#msad111-B17"
                  - text: ). Our choice to confine our analysis to the data we directly collected from taxonomic type strains may have obscured growth in a few species, but in general, it eliminated the effects of inconsistent conditions and taxonomical error.
                - paragraph [ref=e472]:
                  - text: Whereas it remains unclear why
                  - emphasis [ref=e473]: XYL

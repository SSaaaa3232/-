                - paragraph [ref=e95]:
                  - text: The booming field of synthetic biology and metabolic engineering provides promising approaches for sustainable manufacturing of chemicals from renewable feedstocks with microbial cell factories. Classical metabolic engineering strategies mainly focus on altering gene expression levels and enzyme concentrations to improve the metabolic fluxes of specific pathways. However, the impact and limitations of enzyme properties, which are usually ignored in classical metabolic engineering efforts, can hinder further optimization of microbial cell factories. Protein engineering and directed evolution are powerful tools for modifying proteins to achieve desirable properties, and they have been integrated into metabolic engineering efforts to build highly efficient metabolic pathways and optimal industrial chassis. In this review, we present traditional and data-driven strategies and techniques of directed evolution, including random library design, semirational design, smart library design, and
                  - emphasis [ref=e96]: in vivo
                  - text: continuous evolution. We also discuss how these directed evolution strategies have been applied in metabolic engineering toward superphenotypes that cannot be achieved through simple gene overexpression or knockout. Finally, we discuss the challenges of applying protein engineering in metabolic engineering and the prospects for accelerating the directed evolution workflow using the state-of-art technologies.

                - paragraph [ref=e113]:
                  - text: The traditional chemical manufacturing industry faces significant challenges, including environmental pollution, high energy consumption, and resource waste. Building sustainable bioprocesses using microbial cell factories, powered by advances in synthetic biology and metabolic engineering, offers a promising alternative to chemical manufacturing. Over the past few decades, notable efforts have been made to engineer microorganisms for the production of chemicals from renewable feedstocks. Examples include the use of methylotrophic yeast for fatty acid production,
                  - emphasis [ref=e114]: ","
                  - emphasis [ref=e115]: Saccharomyces cerevisiae
                  - text: for vaccine adjuvant QS-21 biosynthesis, and
                  - emphasis [ref=e116]: Escherichia coli
                  - text: for 1,3-propanediol production. However, the successful commercialization of microbe-derived chemicals is still challenging due to the unsatisfying titers, rates, and yields.
                - paragraph [ref=e117]:
                  - text: Adapting microbes for industrial chemical production requires extensive engineering of cell metabolism. Many metabolic strategies have been developed to boost cellular flux through the desired biosynthetic pathways. Developments in systems biology and genome-scale metabolic modeling facilitate the identification of nonintuitive targets for strain optimization. However, most metabolic engineering strategies give rise to changes in enzyme concentrations within cells or subcellular compartments, often neglecting the inherent catalytic properties of enzymes, which can pose significant obstacles to further improvements. In addition, there are always gaps in novel metabolic pathways and regulation networks that need to be filled with enzymes with new functions. Therefore, protein engineering as an essential tool for developing efficient microbial cell factories is indispensable. Directed evolution, a protein engineering strategy, involves iterative rounds of mutation and selection or screening to modify protein properties, and has been broadly applied in metabolic engineering studies.

                  - paragraph [ref=e135]:
                    - text: Error-prone polymerase chain reaction (epPCR) is one of the most popular methods for creating mutant libraries owing to its simplicity and minimum requirements for prior knowledge about the targeted proteins (
                    - link "Figure" [ref=e136] [cursor=pointer]:
                      - /url: "#fig2"
                    - text: a). epPCR has been employed to evolve various protein properties, including substrate specificity, catalytic activity, cofactor specificity, regioselectivity, enantioselectivity, and organic solvent resistance. Despite the simplicity and broad application, potential users should consider several factors before conducting the experiments. First, the size of the library is influenced by the assay technique and mutation rate. Rather than introducing all 19 remaining amino acids at a single position, epPCR allows limited amino acid exchanges, depending on the original codon. Additionally, the mutational bias of DNA polymerases can further restrict the diversity of the mutant library.

                  - heading "Semirational Library Design" [level=3] [ref=e154]
                  - paragraph [ref=e155]: Although random mutagenesis methods can evolve proteins in the absence of structural information, they usually lead to large mutant libraries and require substantial laboratory work, urging high-throughput screening or selection methods. To mitigate these challenges, semirational evolution strategies that utilize information on protein sequences and structure–function relationships have been developed, enabling the generation of less extensive and focused libraries. The rapid progress in the field of artificial intelligence also empowered the modeling of sequence-function and structure–function relationships for protein design.
                  - paragraph [ref=e156]:
                    - text: Multiple sequence alignment (MSA) is a widely applied method based on sequence analysis for functional region prediction and phylogenetic analysis (
                    - link "Figure" [ref=e157] [cursor=pointer]:
                      - /url: "#fig2"
                    - text: b). A great number of MSA-based methods have been developed to improve enzyme properties. For example, ConSurf is a web server that recognizes evolutionary patterns of biological sequences to reveal structurally and/or functionally important positions. In protein engineering studies, ConSurf is usually used in combination with other hotspot prediction methods to exclude highly conserved residues in case of function loss or poor folding.
                    - emphasis [ref=e158]: −
                    - text: MSA also gives information on coevolving positions that have interdependent amino acid frequencies or similar patterns of amino acid substitutions. Such evolutionary pairs give valuable hints of how proteins maintain stability, function, and folding while adapting to selective pressures and can be selected as hot spots for direct evolution. Coevolving-site saturation mutagenesis has been utilized to evolve enzyme properties including thermostability and activity.
                    - emphasis [ref=e159]: −
                    - text: Notably, coevolving sites are not necessarily located in, or close to, the active site; hence, this method enables the identification of hotspots across the entire enzyme molecule.
                  - paragraph [ref=e160]: Another application of MSA involves inferring phylogenetic relationship between homologous sequences for ancestral sequence reconstruction, which is the basis of ancestral library construction for directed evolution. The reconstructed evolutionary adaptive path (REAP) analysis identifies mutated sites responsible for functional divergence along the evolution history for variants library construction. The assumption is that mutations in the identified sites of the targeted protein can lead to properties of interest that exist in the members of the targeted protein family without destroying the core fold and function. The REAP method was applied to construct a functionally enriched variant library to evolve DNA polymerases for an expanded substrate scope. A similar method also adopts ancestral inference for constructing efficient libraries. The difference between these two methods is that, rather than only focusing on function divergence nodes, the latter method includes nodes throughout phylogeny, thus enabling the generation of novel properties that have not existed in ancestors. Ancestral sequence reconstruction can provide probability distribution for the amino acid identity at each position of a given sequence for creating combinatorial libraries, which can be large and difficult to characterize. To reduce the testing effort, machine learning models can be built based on screening data from a portion of the library and applied to predict desired mutants.
                  - paragraph [ref=e161]:
                    - text: Protein structures provide valuable guidance for the design of functionally enriched mutant libraries. Structures determined by X-ray crystallography, NMR and cryo-electron microscopy can be accessed via the Protein Data Bank. For proteins lacking experimentally determined structures, computational tools such as MODELER, SWISS-MODEL, Rosetta, and AlphaFold can assist in modeling or prediction. Typically, structure-guided directed evolution targets mutations in promising regions that greatly influence the catalytic properties of enzymes (
                    - link "Figure" [ref=e162] [cursor=pointer]:
                      - /url: "#fig2"
                    - text: b). The active site of an enzyme binds substrates and creates an optimized microenvironment for the enzymatic reaction, making mutagenesis in this region particularly impactful. Reetz and co-workers developed a strategy called “Combinatorial active-site saturation test” (CAST), which utilizes structural information to rationally select and group residues lining the active site into several sets, within which residues are spatially proximal. Then, site-saturation mutagenesis is performed on each set, either in a single round or iteratively (Iterative saturation mutagenesis, ISM). This approach allows for efficient exploration of the chemical space in active sites through simultaneous randomization at rationally selected multiple sites. To further reduce the screening workload, the amino acid alphabets introduced at each hotspot can be optimized by rational design.
                    - emphasis [ref=e163]: ","
                    - text: CAST and its derivative methods have successfully evolved enzyme properties, including activity, stereo-, and regio-selectivity and substrate scope.
                  - paragraph [ref=e164]:
                    - text: Another crucial region affecting the catalytic properties of enzymes with buried active sites is the access tunnel, which connects the active site to the surrounding environment. According to the “keyhole-lock-key” model, substrate recognition occurs in the access tunnels before it fits into the active site. Cofactors, solvents, and products are also transported through the tunnels, underscoring their importance in the whole catalytic cycle.
                    - emphasis [ref=e165]: ","
                    - text: Replacing bulky residues lining tunnels with smaller ones is a common method to reduce potential steric hindrance and improve ligands transportation. For example, to expand the substrate entry tunnel of a carbonyl reductase, Yan et al. adopted a two-step strategy termed “opening the door” and “expanding the alley”. The resultant variant exhibited an increased activity up to 93 times and an enantiomeric excess (
                    - emphasis [ref=e166]: ee
                    - text: ) value greater than 99.5% (
                    - emphasis [ref=e167]: R
                    - text: ). Additionally, the shape of the tunnels is also important. Analysis of the structure of an amidase indicated that a twisted access tunnel may cause a low ligand transfer efficiency. Mutations aimed at smoothing the shape of the tunnel and reducing steric hindrance resulted in a triple mutant with improved enzyme activity. The reaction rates of different chemical reactions catalyzed by promiscuous enzymes can be affected by the substrate accessibility. Modifying water tunnels to facilitate hydrogen peroxide access to the heme center of P450 monooxygenase CYP199A4 resulted in a remarkable 183-fold increase in peroxygenase activity. Detailed introduction to enzyme access tunnel engineering can be found in the review article from Kokkonen et al.

                  - heading "Smart Library Design" [level=3] [ref=e180]
                  - paragraph [ref=e181]:
                    - text: Recent advances in artificial intelligence have also inspired the incorporation of machine learning into the directed evolution workflow. In conventional directed evolution experiments, the best variants are fixed as parents for the next round of evolution until a suitable level of desired property is achieved. This iterative approach can reduce the screening burden but easily leads to a local optimum. Machine learning models can utilize sequences and screening data of all the variants, including unimproved ones, to learn the inherent patterns and generate predictive models for smart library design, thus accelerating the design-building-testing-learning pipeline. Wu et al. applied random mutagenesis to a set of residues of
                    - emphasis [ref=e182]: Rhodothermus marinus
                    - text: nitric oxide dioxygenase (76%
                    - emphasis [ref=e183]: ee
                    - text: for the (
                    - emphasis [ref=e184]: S
                    - text: )-enantiomer) and randomly selected variants for sampling and training supervised machine learning models for predicting mutants with high selectivity. After two rounds of evolution and a total of 805 variants tested, two variants with enantioselectivities of 93% and 79%
                    - emphasis [ref=e185]: ee
                    - text: for the (
                    - emphasis [ref=e186]: S
                    - text: )- and (
                    - emphasis [ref=e187]: R
                    - text: )-enantiomers were obtained. To improve the activity of acyl-ACP reductase for fatty alcohol production, a property that cannot be assayed in high-throughput, a machine-learning driven approach consisting of generating initial sequence sampling by gene shuffling, training the Gaussian process model, and iterative design-test-learn cycles driven by an upper-confidence bound criterion was proposed. After 10 rounds of evolution, a variant producing over 2-fold more in vivo fatty alcohol than the starting enzyme was obtained. Successful combination of machine learning and structure-based directed evolution has also been demonstrated in the engineering of a serotonin sensor, in which traditional computational modeling was used for binding-pocket redesign, followed by machine learning-guided iterative rounds of SSM. Further optimization in training set design can improve the performance of machine learning models in searching for functioning variants in the large protein sequence space. Recently, active learning, a machine learning paradigm that can leverage uncertainty quantification, was adopted to assist in directed evolution. Larger protein design spaces can be explored by active learning than by supervised learning.

Generative models trained on natural sequences can provide a probabilistic model to capture the effects of mutations in natural evolution, offering potentially beneficial mutations. By sampling mutations situated in the active center of Renilla luciferase, only 8 variants were tested and a M185L mutant with 2 times relative activity to the wild type was obtained.

              - generic [ref=e215]:
                - heading "Integrating Directed Evolution with Metabolic Engineering" [level=2] [ref=e216]: Integrating Directed Evolution with Metabolic Engineering
                - paragraph [ref=e217]: Metabolic engineering aims to enhance the production of targeted metabolites by rewiring cellular metabolism and/or introducing heterologous pathways. Over the past decades, numerous strategies have been proposed to optimize microbial cell factories, including optimizing gene expression levels through promoter, terminator, and transcription factor engineering, engineering cofactor systems for maintaining redox balance and providing driving forces, engineering product transportation between organelles or out of cells, and adaptive laboratory evolution. In addition, systems biology-based mathematical models can point out nonintuitive targets for metabolic engineering. However, most of the strategies mentioned above are based on permanent genetic modifications that may drain cellular resources and hinder cell growth. In contrast, protein engineering stands out as a promising strategy that deals with the efficiency and/or promiscuity of enzymatic activities in microbial cell factories, allowing for enhancing fluxes in targeted pathways without great investments in cellular proteome.

                - heading "Conclusions and Prospects" [level=2] [ref=e399]: Conclusions and Prospects
                - paragraph [ref=e400]: Protein engineering and directed evolution have been extensively applied to improve enzyme-based industrial processes. Numerous methods and techniques have been proposed to improve protein properties, regardless of prior knowledge of the targeted proteins. Fast-developing artificial intelligence has also empowered the field of directed evolution. As the basic components of microbial cell factories, proteins are critical targets for modification, especially when their properties are unsatisfying. Efforts to develop efficient microbial cell factories have given rise to powerful and sophisticated metabolic engineering strategies, which can be summarized by a three-dimensional model. The integration of protein engineering and directed evolution within this framework enables molecular-level modifications for enhancing, regulating, and innovating metabolic pathways and optimizing microbial chassis.

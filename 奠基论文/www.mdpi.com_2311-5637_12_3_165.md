## Abstract
Cofactors are small molecules or ions that participate in enzymatic reactions as essential carriers of electrons, atoms, or functional groups, thereby governing cellular redox balance and energy metabolism. In the yeast Saccharomyces cerevisiae, the availability of cofactors such as NAD(H), NADP(H), CoA, and acetyl-CoA directly influences the flux through biosynthetic pathways leading to aroma-active compounds. Esters and higher alcohols are the two most important families of volatile flavor compounds in fermented alcoholic beverages. Their synthesis is intimately linked to the intracellular levels and ratios of these cofactors. This review summarizes recent progress in cofactor engineering strategies aimed at modulating the production of esters, higher alcohols, and 2,3-butanediol in S. cerevisiae. We discuss the underlying metabolic pathways, highlight key studies that manipulate cofactor pools to redirect carbon flux, and examine emerging tools (e.g., riboswitches, fine-tuned promoter systems) that enable precise cofactor balancing. Finally, we outline future challenges and opportunities for applying cofactor engineering to design yeast cell factories with tailored flavor profiles.

##  1\. Background
Saccharomyces cerevisiae is the cornerstone of the food and beverage industry, driving alcoholic fermentation and shaping the sensory character of wine, beer, and spirits [1]. In recent years, with advances in synthetic biology, S. cerevisiae has been further engineered as a “cell factory” for the biosynthesis of aroma compounds such as esters and higher alcohols, as well as high-value bio-flavor molecules like terpenes and steroids, significantly enhancing its potential in natural fragrance production [2,3]. Its ease of genetic manipulation, well-characterized metabolic network, and availability of comprehensive models make it an ideal microbial species for industrial biotechnology applications. Given the central role of S. cerevisiae in these industries, its metabolic activity not only determines fermentation efficiency and product stability but also directly shapes the final flavor profile. During fermentation, yeast synthesizes a range of volatile compounds through a complex metabolic network, among which esters and higher alcohols are widely recognized as key aroma compounds that impart distinctive fruity and floral notes to wine, beer, and other fermented beverages due to their low odor thresholds and high aroma activity [4]. Consequently, elucidating and modulating biosynthetic pathways of these two chemical classes in yeast has become a major research focus for improving product quality and flavor consistency.
The global alcohol market continues to expand; concurrently, consumer preferences have evolved beyond classic flavor profiles toward options that are low-calorie, low-fusel, fruit-forward, or designed for “next-day clarity”. Meeting these demands requires tighter, more precise steering of yeast metabolism. Alcoholic beverages contain over a thousand volatile constituents, yet it is the esters and higher alcohols that sit in the driver’s seat of consumer buying preference [5]. Analyses of multiple alcoholic beverages reveal that the relative abundance of esters and higher alcohols is the foremost determinant of product quality—far exceeding the contributions of ethanol, residual sugar or acidity [6]. Consequently, strain-design programmers in the alcoholic-beverage industry now center on a single core objective: engineering S. cerevisiae that overproduce esters while keeping higher-alcohol levels below their sensory threshold. Traditional genetic strategies—such as overexpressing alcohol acetyl-transferases ATF1 and EEB1 or deleting branched-chain amino acid transaminase BAT2 often fail at industrial scale because the supply of cofactors (acetyl-CoA, NAD(P)H, ATP, SAM, CoA, Mg2+) dictates the final flavor spectrum rather than enzyme dosage per se [7]. As research advances, cofactor engineering has moved from a niche topic to the mainstream of fermentation in yeast. This review summarizes recent progress in modulating ester and higher alcohol synthesis through cofactor engineering in S. cerevisiae, providing a knowledge base for future strain improvement and for meeting the growing demand for personalized, premium alcoholic beverages. Cofactors are non-protein compounds that bind to enzymes and are essential for catalytic reactions, typically including organic molecules (such as coenzymes) and metal ions. They play important roles in transferring electrons, atoms, or groups during the catalytic process of enzymes [8]. Some cofactors can also maintain the three-dimensional structure of enzymes to remain their activity. In S. cerevisiae, cofactors such as NAD(P)H/NAD(P)+, acetyl-CoA, and ATP/ADP regulate key enzymes in metabolic pathways to participate in the synthesis and catabolism of substances [9]. As key redox cofactors, nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide (NAD+) and nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide phosphate (NADP+) function as electron donors or acceptors in metabolic and biosynthetic processes, existing in oxidized forms (NAD+/NADP+) and reduced forms (NADH/NADPH), respectively [10]. They play distinct biochemical functions in microbial life and metabolic activities. Coenzyme A (CoA) is another essential cofactor for numerous enzymes involved in acyl group transfer. It plays a critical role in the catabolism of sugars and fatty acids, as well as in the biosynthesis of sterols, fatty acids, and polyketides across all living organisms [11]. Its derivative, acetyl-CoA, is required for histone acetylation in eukaryotic chromatin and serves as a key C2 substrate for gluconeogenesis in many microorganisms [12]. Synthesis of acetyl-CoA mainly occurs in the cytoplasm and mitochondria of S. cerevisiae. Cofactors are capable of modulating the intracellular redox state, regulating energy metabolism, and directing carbon flux, among other functions [13]. Therefore, cofactor manipulation impacts numerous biochemical reactions, resulting that cofactor engineering is a useful strategy to increase the metabolic pathway efficiency and maximize metabolic flux toward target products.
Cofactor engineering can carefully monitor the balance of NADH/NAD+, NADPH/NADP+ and acetyl-CoA/CoA ratio, overcoming the bottleneck of cofactor availability in dependent biosynthetic pathways by establishing cellular redox balance, thus improving the physiological state of the cell factories. The development of versatile cofactor engineering strategies has markedly accelerated progress in metabolic engineering, enabling significant achievements in amino acid biosynthesis and beyond [14,15,16,17], natural product [18,19,20] and biofuels production [21], as well as substrate utilization, such as xylose [22,23] in industrial microorganisms (Figure 1). Cofactor engineering can also precisely channels cellular carbon flux toward target flavor molecules (esters and higher alcohols) by dynamically tuning the concentration, ratio, and subcellular distribution of intracellular cofactors, thereby increasing the yield and production efficiency of the target products [24,25,26].
In industrial biotechnology, cofactor engineering has become an important means of optimizing microbial cell factories, contributing to efficient, green, and sustainable biological production. Here, we review the recent advances in the synthesis of esters and alcohols of S. cerevisiae, including the biosynthetic pathways of esters, higher alcohols, and 2.3-butanediol, the research progress in cofactor engineering applied to modulate esters, higher alcohols, and 2.3-butanediol, and the strategies for application of cofactor engineering to modulate target products in S. cerevisiae. Finally, further perspectives are proposed on the application of cofactor engineering for metabolic product synthesis in S. cerevisiae cells.
##  2\. Biosynthesis of Esters, Higher Alcohols and 2.3-Butanediol in S. cerevisiae
In the fermentation process, flavor compounds are the result of metabolic activity of yeast cells and other microbial cells [27]. Esters and higher alcohols, which constitute the most abundant and important groups of volatile flavor compounds in fermented alcoholic beverages, are significantly important for the quality and flavor profiles. Meanwhile, most esters are widely used in the food, pharmaceutical, and cosmetics industries, owing to their favorable hygroscopic and emulsifying properties [28]. As nontoxic, biodegradable liquids, they have recently been characterized as green solvents with the potential to replace toxic solvents in a wide range of industrial applications [29,30]. Higher alcohols, also known as fusel alcohols, not only greatly influence the organoleptic quality and character of alcoholic beverage, but are also environmentally friendly biofuels with a high octane number used in fuel industry. In yeast-fermented alcoholic beverages, esters and higher alcohols are the two key secondary metabolites that dictate fruity, floral aromas and “body” perception. They share the upstream metabolic pool of “sugar–amino acid–acyl-CoA”, yet their biosynthetic routes, rate-limiting enzymes, and regulatory nodes differ markedly.
###  2.1. Biosynthetic Pathways of Esters
Flavor-active esters in fermented beverages are primarily divided into two categories. The first comprises acetate esters, where the acid moiety is acetate and the alcohol moiety is ethanol or a higher alcohol derived from amino acid metabolism; typical examples include ethyl acetate, isoamyl acetate, isobutyl acetate, and phenylethyl acetate. The second category consists of medium-chain fatty acid (MCFA) ethyl esters, characterized by an ethanol alcohol moiety and a medium-chain fatty acid moiety, such as ethyl hexanoate and ethyl octanoate [31]. These esters show different flavors and have different thresholds in different alcoholic beverages. Because acetate esters are produced at significantly higher concentrations and are easier to measure, they have received the most research attention in the past.
The synthesis of volatile esters is crucial for aroma contribution in alcoholic beverage fermentations by S. cerevisiae, and they diverge from cytosolic acetyl-CoA, which come from glycolysis’s end-product pyruvate [32]. For acetate esters such as isoamyl acetate, this cytosolic acetyl-CoA directly serves as the acyl donor. As shown in Figure 2, it is condensed with higher alcohols (e.g., isopentanol, isobutanol, 2-phenylethanol) by the action of alcohol acetyltransferases ATF1 and ATF2 [33]. Concurrently, the same cytosolic acetyl-CoA pool drives the synthesis of medium-chain fatty acid (MCFA) ethyl esters (Figure 2). In this pathway, acetyl-CoA is first carboxylated to malonyl-CoA by acetyl-CoA carboxylase (ACC1). Subsequently, the fatty acid synthase (FAS) complex uses acetyl-CoA and malonyl-CoA to generate long-chain fatty acids (LCFAs). These LCFAs are imported into peroxisomes, where incomplete β-oxidation shortens their chains, resulting in the accumulation of MCFA precursors—including hexanoic (C6), octanoic (C8), and decanoic (C10) acids. Concurrently, ethanol is produced en masse from pyruvate via acetaldehyde. The cytosolic MCFAs are first activated to their coenzyme A thioesters (MCFA-CoAs) by acyl-CoA synthetases, and finally, alcohol acyltransferases Eeb1 and Eht1 catalyze the esterification of these MCFA-CoAs with ethanol [34].
A fundamental principle governing the synthesis of both acetate esters and MCFA ethyl esters is the reversibility of the ester-forming reactions. However, the high volatility of these esters leads to their continuous physical removal from the aqueous phase and subsequent accumulation in the fermenter headspace. This constant depletion of the products effectively shifts the thermodynamic equilibrium, driving net ester synthesis and ultimately defining the complex fruity and floral aroma profile of the final beverage.
###  2.2. Biosynthetic Pathways of Higher Alcohols and 2.3-Butanediol
The key sensory-active higher alcohols in alcoholic beverages include n-propanol, isobutanol, isopentanol, and 2-phenylethanol [35]. The concentrations of these compounds in S. cerevisiae are closely linked to amino acid metabolism, given that amino acids are the main assimilable nitrogen source [36]. Among the higher alcohols—such as n-butanol, 3-methyl-1-butanol, n-hexanol, and n-octanol—those with dual functionality as fuels and chemical feedstocks are especially noteworthy for their compatibility with current infrastructure [37].
Higher alcohols are primarily generated from amino acid catabolism (Leu, Val, Ile, Phe) via the Ehrlich pathway, with contributions from glycolytic α-keto acids [35] (Figure 2). In contrast, 2,3-butanediol (2,3-BDO) is a dihydric alcohol and serves as an intermediate chemical for synthesizing valuable products used in cosmetics, pharmaceuticals, food additives, and industrial solvents (e.g., antifreeze) [38,39]. It can also act as a precursor for 1,3-butadiene, a monomer in synthetic rubber production. The biosynthesis of 2,3-BDO begins with the glycolytic intermediate pyruvate, which is converted by α-acetolactate synthase (ALS) to α-acetolactate; this compound spontaneously decarboxylates to diacetyl, and is subsequently reduced stepwise to 2,3-BDO by diacetyl reductase (BDH) [39].
###  2.3. Research Progress on Ester and Alcohol Synthesis
“Ester aroma” and “alcohol bouquet” are the core sensory dimensions that determine the quality of alcoholic beverages. As consumers demand ever-higher flavor complexity, research in this field has intensified, the key enzymes and their genes responsible for ester and alcohol synthesis have attracted worldwide attention. Traditional metabolic engineering efforts have successfully identified key genetic levers that shape flavor profiles in S. cerevisiae. For instance, manipulating the acetate ester pathway through overexpression of the alcohol acetyltransferase gene ATF1 combined with deletion of the esterase gene IAH1 dramatically shifts the balance toward fruity acetate esters while suppressing solvent-like higher alcohols, revealing the intrinsic competition between these two classes of compounds [40]. Likewise, enhancing the supply of medium-chain acyl-CoA precursors—either by introducing point mutations (ScFAS1I306A and ScFAS2G1250S [41]) in fatty acid synthase genes or by overexpressing acyltransferases (EHT1, EEB1) [42]—has proven effective in elevating ethyl ester levels in both sake and lager yeasts, underscoring the importance of precursor availability for ester biosynthesis. Beyond esters, targeted deletions in higher alcohol and 2,3-butanediol pathways (e.g., ADH2, THI3, BDH1) combined with overexpression of ILV5 have successfully reduced off-flavors and improved sensory quality in pilot-scale fermentations [43]. Collectively, these studies demonstrate that while single-gene interventions can profoundly influence flavor compound production, they also reveal a fundamental limitation: the magnitude and direction of metabolic flux are ultimately governed by the intracellular availability of cofactors such as acetyl-CoA, NAD(P)H, and CoA. Although traditional overexpression or deletion strategies enable directional tuning of ester and alcohol levels, they rarely achieve synchronous fine-tuning of both classes. This realization has catalyzed the emergence of cofactor engineering as a more integrated approach to orchestrate yeast metabolism, enabling the in-situ re-balancing of NAD(P)H, acetyl-CoA, and CoA pools to channel carbon efficiently and specifically toward desired esters or higher alcohols—a level of precision that single-gene modifications alone cannot achieve.
##  3\. Applications of Cofactor Engineering in Production of Esters, Higher Alcohols and 2.3-Butanediol
For regulating ester or/and higher alcohol synthesis in yeast fermentation, a different pathway has been conducted in yeast S. cerevisiae. Cofactor engineering has been employed as an effective strategy to enhance the efficiency of metabolic pathways and direct metabolic flux toward target products, including the regulation of ester and higher alcohol synthesis in S. cerevisiae.
###  3.1. Applications of Cofactor Engineering to Modulate Ester Production
The core of cofactor engineering for ester biosynthesis lies in the synergistic design and systemic regulation of two key cofactor networks—the acyl carrier network (centered on CoA/acetyl-CoA) and the redox network (represented by NAD(P)H/NAD(P)+)—to overcome multiple bottlenecks in precursor supply, energy balance, and reducing power drive. Ester synthesis is essentially the condensation of acyl-CoA (derived from CoA) and alcohols catalyzed by alcohol acyltransferases, the efficiency of which is directly controlled by the concentration and specificity of acyl-CoA. Meanwhile, the generation of these precursors (e.g., through the fatty acid synthesis pathway to extend the acetyl-CoA chain) and the maintenance of cellular homeostasis are highly dependent on the reducing power provided by NAD(P)H. The following text and Table 1 summarize some cofactor regulation strategies used in ester synthesis.
####  3.1.1. Metabolic Impact of CoA/Acetyl-CoA Perturbations on the Biosynthesis of Esters
Esters are synthesized intracellularly through a reaction catalyzed by acyl transferase or “ester synthase,” a process that requires the involvement of CoA or acetyl-CoA [32]. Therefore, it is necessary to regulate not only gene expression but also cofactor levels when regulating ester synthesis. To prove that the CoA/acetyl-CoA manipulation system can be used to increase the productivity of industrially useful compounds derived from acetyl-CoA, Vadali et al. engineered an E. coli strain with elevated intracellular levels of CoA/acetyl-CoA, which resulted in increased isoamyl acetate production [44]. In this study, isoamyl acetate production was further enhanced through an integrated cofactor engineering strategy, which combined the expression of the ATF2 gene (encoding alcohol acetyltransferase), increased intracellular CoA/acetyl-CoA levels via panK overexpression, and deletion of competing pathways. These results demonstrated that regulating the concentrations of intracellular CoA and acetyl-CoA leads to an increase in isoamyl acetate production.
To investigate the relationship between branched-chain amino acid uptake, intracellular CoA/acetyl-CoA levels, and acetate ester synthesis, taking into account both the consecutive and endogenous pathways of CoA biosynthesis. As pantothenate kinase Cab1 (encoded by CAB1) has been identified as the rate-limiting enzyme in CoA biosynthesis [51], its overexpression in yeast and E. coli can enhance pantothenate kinase activity in the consecutive pathway [52]. To enhance intracellular CoA and acetyl-CoA availability, we overexpressed CAB1 and CAB3, two key genes in the CoA biosynthetic pathway, and compared their effects with those of BAP2 deletion—a gene involved in branched-chain amino acid permeation that indirectly influences endogenous CoA metabolism. All modifications were combined with ATF1 overexpression to boost acetate ester synthesis [25]. Analysis of the engineered strains revealed distinct shifts in acetyl-CoA/CoA and NAD(H) pools. While BAP2 deletion alone had a modest impact on ester production, overexpression of CAB1 or CAB3 substantially increased both ethyl acetate and isoamyl acetate levels, confirming that enhancing CoA biosynthesis is an effective strategy to promote ester formation. The observed elevation in NAD(H) levels further suggested broader metabolic reorganization, likely reflecting altered flux through interconnected pathways. Building on these findings, we next pursued a more integrated engineering strategy to channel carbon toward ethyl acetate by simultaneously enhancing catalytic efficiency and cofactor supply [50]. Using the PGK1 promoter and terminator to coordinately regulate CAB2, ACS2 (acetyl-CoA synthetase), and ATF1, we constructed the strain CLy12a-ATF1-ACS2-CAB2, which achieved a marked increase in ethyl acetate yield [50]. Although cofactor levels were not directly measured in this iteration, the work underscores the potential of combinatorial metabolic engineering to improve flavor compound production and by-product utilization, offering a promising foundation for both enhancing wine quality and advancing industrial-scale ethyl acetate biosynthesis.
Beyond direct genetic manipulation of CoA biosynthetic genes, an alternative layer of metabolic control operates through the carnitine acetyltransferase (CrAT) system, which dynamically buffers acetyl-CoA and CoA pools across subcellular compartments. In S. cerevisiae, the CAT2 gene encodes the predominant CrAT isoform, localized in both peroxisomes and mitochondria, and accounts for the vast majority of total CrAT activity [53]. By catalyzing the reversible transfer of acetyl groups between CoA and carnitine, Cat2p modulates the availability of acetyl-CoA in distinct organelles. Building on this understanding, Cordente and colleagues explored whether redirecting Cat2p activity could influence ester synthesis during fermentation [45]. They overexpressed either the native mitochondrial form (Cat2pmit) or an engineered cytosolic variant (Cat2pcyt), hypothesizing that altering the subcellular distribution of acetyl-CoA would impact ester production. Both modifications led to a general reduction in ester levels, with distinct patterns: cytosolic Cat2p overexpression more strongly suppressed ethyl acetate, while mitochondrial targeting had a greater effect on isoamyl acetate [45]. These observations suggest that overexpression of Cat2p may shift the equilibrium toward CoA formation via mass action, thereby limiting acyl-CoA precursors available for ester biosynthesis. More broadly, this work highlights the carnitine shuttle as a mechanistically distinct and spatially precise tool for tuning acetyl-CoA/CoA homeostasis, offering a complementary strategy to conventional pathway engineering for modulating flavor compound production in yeast.
Moreover, a parallel strategy focuses on engineering the pathways that supply acyl-CoA precursors for ester synthesis. In S. cerevisiae, the formation of medium-chain fatty acid ethyl esters (FAEEs), such as ethyl hexanoate, octanoate, and decanoate, depends on a coordinated cascade involving aldehyde dehydrogenase (Ald6), acetyl-CoA synthetase (Acs1), acetyl-CoA carboxylase (Acc1), and the fatty acid synthase complex (Fas1/Fas2). Recognizing that the availability of acetyl-CoA and malonyl-CoA limits flux toward these esters, Shi and colleagues engineered a strain overexpressing ALD6, ACS1, and a feedback-resistant variant of ACC1 to expand these precursor pools. When combined with heterologous expression of strawberry alcohol acyltransferase (SAAT), which efficiently condenses C6–C10 acyl-CoAs with ethanol, this strategy resulted in a substantial enhancement of FAEE production [34]. Complementing this precursor-push approach, targeted modification of the fatty acid synthase complex itself has also proven effective. By applying CRISPR-Cas9 editing to introduce specific point mutations in FAS1 and FAS2 in lager yeast, researchers were able to selectively increase the pools of hexanoyl- and octanoyl-CoA, thereby channeling flux toward the corresponding ethyl esters [42]. Beyond these genetic interventions, the efficiency of the final ester-forming step can also be augmented: overexpression of the acyltransferases EHT1p and EEB1p, which catalyze the condensation of acyl-CoAs with ethanol, has been shown to elevate FAEE levels [46]. Finally, fermentation conditions themselves can influence precursor supply; for instance, supplementation with L-phenylalanine or L-leucine in simulated grape juice enhances key enzyme activities and elevates acetyl-CoA levels, promoting the synthesis of character-impact esters such as phenethyl acetate and isoamyl acetate [47]. FAEE production can be enhanced through multiple, complementary levers—ranging from upstream precursor expansion and FAS complex engineering to downstream transferase overexpression and fermentation optimization, all of which converge on the central goal of increasing acyl-CoA availability and utilization.
####  3.1.2. Metabolic Effects of Redox Cofactor Perturbations on Ester Biosynthesis
Redox homeostasis is essential for sustaining metabolism, energy production, and cellular growth. As central hubs in metabolic networks, the redox cofactors NADH and NADPH exhibit extensive connectivity, and fluctuations in their concentrations can broadly influence ester biosynthesis. To separately examine the effects of NADPH or NADH imbalance on aroma compound formation during wine fermentation, strains overexpressing either native NADH-dependent or engineered NADPH-dependent butanediol dehydrogenase (Bdh) were constructed and cultured in chemically defined medium. By adjusting the concentration of supplemented acetoin, this biological system allows precise modulation of the NAD(P)/NAD(P)H balance. Fermentations with 59A-NADH-Bdh and 59A-NADPH-Bdh strains, grown without the addition of 200 mM acetoin, served as respective controls for analyzing NADH- and NADPH-specific perturbations. Different esters produced in these strains have different reactions under different conditions. For example, the concentrations of some esters (ethyl acetate, ethyl propionate, propyl acetate, and isobutyl acetate) in the two strains have increased significantly, three-fold higher than that in the control condition [48]. However, other ester levels in the two strains are irregular; some MCFA esters were increased or decreased significantly under the same conditions. For instance, in wine fermentation, controlled adjustment of the extracellular oxidation–reduction potential (ORP) to approximately 0 mV was found to optimize the NADP+/NADPH pool, upregulating key genes (e.g., ACC1, FAS1) in the fatty acid synthesis pathway and consequently enhancing the production of desirable medium-chain fatty acid ethyl esters [49]. In fermented fruit beverages, yeasts and other microorganisms are key to the production of flavor compounds such as esters, and their metabolic processes are closely linked to intracellular redox reactions [54]. The changes in the redox status of S. cerevisiae cells have a wide range of effects on the formation of esters and therefore may serve as convenient leverage for the modulation of aroma compound production. However, implementation of such an approach still requires a better understanding of the mechanisms underlying the responses of yeast to variations in the redox balance.
###  3.2. Cofactor Engineering Used to Modulate Higher Alcohols Production
The Ehrlich pathway for higher alcohol synthesis from amino acids is traditionally viewed as amino acid-centric. Its flux is fundamentally governed by the availability of key cofactors that drive precursor formation and final reduction [55]. The synthesis of the pathway’s initial α-keto acid precursors heavily relies on acetyl-CoA pools for carbon skeleton assembly in branched-chain amino acid biosynthesis (Figure 2). More directly, the final, irreversible reduction step of aldehydes to their corresponding higher alcohols is strictly dependent on NAD(P)H as the reducing equivalent. Therefore, engineering strategies that expand the cytosolic acetyl-CoA supply can enhance precursor availability, while simultaneously manipulating the NADPH/NADP+ ratio. This integrated cofactor engineering approach enables precise control over the delicate balance of desirable fruity alcohols and undesirable solvent-like off-notes, offering a powerful tool for tailoring the flavor profiles of beverages such as beer, wine, and sake at a metabolic root level.
Alcohol and acetyl CoA are the precursors to synthesize esters under the catalytic reaction of alcohol acetyltransferases, so the corresponding higher alcohol level will also change when the ester level changes (Figure 2). When investigating the relationship between branched-chain amino acid uptake, intracellular CoA/acetyl-CoA levels, and acetate ester synthesis, the results revealed a shift in higher alcohol profiles: levels of n-propanol, methyl-1-butanol, and isopentanol decreased, while levels of isobutanol and phenethyl alcohol increased [25]. Our other work was to increase ethyl acetate production by regulating ethyl acetate metabolic pathway and intracellular CoA and acetyl-CoA levels, and the production of higher alcohols also showed a different degree of decline [50]. We hypothesize that these changes may be attributed to altered uptake of branched-chain amino acids in BAP2 deletion strains: reduced leucine, isoleucine, and valine uptake alongside increased phenylalanine and threonine uptake. The decreased valine uptake, as observed in prior work, lowered the acetyl-CoA/CoA ratio and certain higher alcohol levels. Additionally, the unblocking of the ethyl acetate pathway allows some higher alcohols to be diverted into ester synthesis (Figure 2), leading to varying degrees of change in total higher alcohol production across both studies.
###  3.3. Cofactor Engineering for 2,3-Butanediol Production
2,3-BDO and its precursor acetoin are key to creamy and cheesy flavors. Fine-tuning their production through cofactor engineering can optimize the overall flavor profile of beverages. Numerous research groups have explored the production of 2,3-butanediol (2,3-BDO) through microbial fermentation using native bacterial producers (Table 2). These bacteria are mainly classified as class II (pathogenic), and S. cerevisiae is widely recognized as a GRAS (Generally Recognized as Safe) organism and serves as a workhorse for producing valuable biochemicals and biofuels, owing to its high tolerance to alcohols and harsh environmental conditions [56,57]. Some state-of-the-art cofactor engineering strategies have been attempted in S. cerevisiae to produce 2,3-BDO. For instance, to assess how Saccharomyces cerevisiae metabolism responds to a reduced intracellular NADH pool, Heux et al. expressed Lactococcus lactis noxE encoding a water-forming NADH oxidase in the S. cerevisiae strain V5 [58]. Water-forming NADH oxidases catalyze the oxidation of NADH to NAD+ with oxygen as the electron acceptor, producing water as a by-product [59,60]. Consequently, the expression of such oxidases reduces the intracellular NADH concentration. According to these results, the Lactococcus lactis noxE gene was expressed in a 2,3-BDO-producing pyruvate decarboxylase-deficient strain. The yield of 2,3-BDO increased by 23.8% compared to the control strain without noxE expression [61]. To further enhance 2,3-BDO production, they also blocked both the ethanol and glycerol pathways and expressed the 2,3-BDO biosynthetic pathway. The resulting strain could produce 73 g/L of 2,3-BDO with 0.41 g/g of yield (equal to 82% of the theoretical yield) [62]. Furthermore, by co-expressing the L. lactis noxE gene and the C. tropicalis PDC1 gene in a pyruvate-decarboxylase-deficient 2,3-BDO-producing strain, a high titer (154.3 g/L) and productivity (1.98 g/L/h) of 2,3-BDO were achieved in fed-batch fermentation [63]. This group also engineered S. cerevisiae for efficient 2,3-butanediol (2,3-BDO) production from xylose by modulating the xylose metabolic pathway and optimizing cofactor levels. Specifically, they expressed Scheffersomyces stipitis transaldolase and an NADH-preferring xylose reductase to enhance xylose consumption. To increase 2,3-BDO yield and overcome C2-dependent growth limitations in a pyruvate-decarboxylase-deficient strain, the Lactococcus lactis noxE and Candida tropicalis PDC1 genes were co-expressed. Fed-batch fermentation with the final strain achieved 96.8 g/L of 2,3-BDO from xylose, representing 15.6-fold improvements in titer, compared to the control [39]. Sugimura et al. significantly increased the specific production rate of 2,3-BDO by deleting the RIM15 gene and activating the pyruvate consumption pathway [64]. This strategy optimizes central carbon metabolism, indirectly affecting metabolite pools including cofactors. Using a combination of Plackett–Burman design, steepest ascent experiments, and Central Composite Design, a study identified acetic acid, peptone, and glucose as the three most significant factors influencing 2,3-BD production by S. cerevisiae L7 [65]. These results suggest that the possibility of using cofactor engineering and metabolic engineering strategies together in yeast to produce valuable 2,3-BDO chemicals. While the studies above demonstrate the feasibility of cofactor engineering in S. cerevisiae under controlled laboratory conditions, achieving high-titer 2,3-butanediol production in industrial media (e.g., crude substrates, lignocellulosic hydrolysates) has thus far been predominantly reported in bacterial systems [66,67,68]. This highlights a promising avenue for future research, leveraging the GRAS status and robust industrial scalability of S. cerevisiae to translate these bacterial-derived strategies into yeast-based industrial applications.
##  4\. Potential Strategies for Cofactor Engineering
To modulate ester or higher alcohol production, many metabolic engineering efforts towards utilization of pathway manipulations have been widely undertaken in S. cerevisiae [32,40], but the large quantity of publications on the cofactor engineering strategies used to regulate ester or higher alcohol synthesis that appeared over the past twenty years testifies to the significance of the subject. In addition, different cofactor engineering strategies were also used to improve the yield of another important alcohol, bioethanol [70,71,72,73], and to promote the use of xylose or other carbon sources [74,75]. The use of various cofactor engineering methods for ester or higher alcohol production improved our understanding and application of cofactor engineering. These strategies are schematically summarized in Figure 3. However, gene expression, fermentation conditions, and cofactor levels have never been completely characterized to explain the mechanisms of synthesis and regulation of these flavor substances. Additional work is also needed to ensure the fine regulation of cofactor levels to reduce the cofactor imbalance. Therefore, further efforts are needed to advance the application of cofactor engineering in enhancing ester and higher alcohol synthesis in yeast.
###  4.1. Optimization of Fermentation Parameters
Different fermentation parameters may have different effects on yeast metabolism and further affect the production of bioproducts. Pires et al. has fully reviewed the effect of fermentation parameters on the biosynthesis of esters or higher alcohols. Yeast strain, culture media, and fermentation conditions each have an influence on flavor substances [76], and previous works also confirmed that the change in amino acid levels in yeast cells might affect acetyl-CoA/CoA levels in the endogenous pathway starting from amino acids [25,77,78]. Our group has successfully modulated acetate ester and higher alcohol production by regulating branched-chain amino acid permeases to alter intracellular CoA and acetyl-CoA levels [25]. Furthermore, to enhance SAM biosynthesis in S. cerevisiae, water-forming NADH oxidase, Vitreoscilla hemoglobin, and phosphite dehydrogenase were employed in combination with SAM2 overexpression to modulate NADH availability and regulate oxygen supply [79]. The final engineered yeast strain achieved a maximum SAM titer of nearly 55 mg/L, representing a 67% increase over the control. These findings highlight that optimizing fermentation parameters is crucial for maintaining cofactor balance, thereby enhancing the production of flavor compounds and other chemicals.
###  4.2. Regulation of Cofactor Level
Current cofactor engineering mainly targets natural metabolic pathways and endogenous enzymes. To achieve optimal cofactor balance for the biosynthesis of flavor compounds and other chemicals, novel metabolic engineering technologies need to be developed. To achieve cofactor balance, enzyme engineering and promoter engineering have been developed to synthesize different bioproducts. For example, Banta et al. performed a series of site-directed mutations at the cofactor binding site of the 2’phosphate group of NADPH to achieve redox balance in 2-keto-Lgulonic acid (2-KLG) fermentation, and the five obtained mutants were able to use both NADH and NADPH as a cofactor. One of the mutants showed a 110-fold increase in activity with NADH compared with that of the wild-type 2,5-DKG, enabling a more efficient one-step fermentation process for 2-KLG production [80]. To fine-tune D-lactate production, a constitutive promoter library was constructed to precisely regulate the expression of Lactococcus lactis NoxE. This approach revealed that the reduced pyruvate flux into the ALS pathway was redirected toward the LDH pathway [81]. Furthermore, to regulate the ethyl acetate yield, we precisely modulated ATF1 expression by seamlessly inserting serially truncated PGK1 promoters—shortened from the 3′ end in 100-bp steps—into the yeast genome. This strategy enabled a gradual increase in ethyl acetate production through fine-tuned control of ATF1 expression [82]. Although these works are not directly applied to cofactor engineering to regulate the synthesis of esters or higher alcohols, the successful implementation of these strategies has important reference significance for regulating their biosynthesis.
###  4.3. Synthetic Biology Tools for Cofactor Balance Regulation
Traditional metabolic engineering typically involves deleting genes in competing pathways to enhance precursor/intermediate availability and overexpressing genes in target pathways to maximize production rates [83]. However, these methods maybe lead to uncertain changes in genotype and phenotype. A riboswitch is a regulatory RNA that controls gene expression through specific ligand binding, making it an attractive genetic tool for conditionally regulating gene expression and metabolic pathways [84]. Recently, Chen et al. developed a cofactor- sensing riboswitch to balance the requirement of cofactor in SAM biosynthesis. In this study, the newly identified ATP-sensing riboswitch ydaO motif was applied to SAM biosynthesis. The results demonstrated that the Bacillus subtilis ydaO element, activated by ATP, can regulate gene expression in E. coli. Moreover, this ydaO-based regulatory strategy elevated both ATP levels and SAM titer relative to the control [85]. Riboswitch elements with similar functions exist in both eukaryotic and prokaryotic cells [84,86]. Moreover, scaffold proteins [87], ribozyme switches, and antisense switches [88] have also proven effective in enhancing the production of valuable chemicals. In the future, these synthetic biology tools could be rationally designed to achieve cofactor balance and ester or higher alcohol production in yeast.
##  5\. Conclusions
Cofactor engineering has emerged as a powerful strategy for simultaneously modulating the synthesis of esters, higher alcohols, and 2,3-butanediol in S. cerevisiae, offering distinct advantages over single-gene approaches by addressing the systemic nature of metabolic networks. Key achievements in this area include enhancing acetate ester production by boosting CoA/acetyl-CoA pools through pantothenate kinase overexpression and carnitine shuttle modulation; increasing medium-chain fatty acid ethyl esters via engineering of fatty acid synthase and acyl-CoA supply; improving 2,3-BDO yields through expression of NADH oxidase to rebalance NAD+/NADH and deletion of competing pathways; and demonstrating that redox cofactor perturbations—via engineered dehydrogenases or oxidation-reduction potential control—can shift ester profiles. Despite this progress, several challenges remain. These include the issue of compartmentalization, as cofactor pools differ between cytosol, mitochondria, and peroxisomes, necessitating organelle-specific enzyme targeting and measurement tools; the need for dynamic control strategies beyond static overexpression or deletion, such as cofactor-responsive biosensors (e.g., riboswitches or transcription factors) that autonomously maintain metabolic balance; the importance of validating laboratory findings in complex industrial substrates (e.g., wort, grape must, molasses), where encouraging preliminary results require further expansion; and the complexity of multi-product optimization, which demands multi-objective cofactor engineering—potentially guided by metabolic models—to achieve desired flavor profiles by simultaneously increasing some esters while decreasing others and maintaining higher alcohols within optimal ranges. Looking ahead, future research should integrate cofactor engineering with synthetic biology tools and systems biology approaches to achieve predictable and robust strain performance. As consumer demand for diverse, high-quality fermented beverages continues to grow, cofactor engineering will play an increasingly central role in designing yeast cell factories with tailored flavor outputs.
